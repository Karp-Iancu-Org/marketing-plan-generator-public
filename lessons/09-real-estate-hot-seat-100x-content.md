# Workshop 09 — Real Estate Hot Seat, Julian Lin's "100x Your Content," and the VIP Offer

**Source:** Acquisition.com/Mozi workshop, Day 2 (08-20) — "Strategic Overhaul of a Family-Run Real Estate Listing Business" transcript.

This session runs in three distinct segments. **Segment A** is a live hot seat with a family-run, flat-fee MLS listing business out of Ohio — four family members, no paid ads, thin margins — and it produces some of the clearest pricing and funnel-architecture prescriptions of the whole workshop: real CAC accounting, the close-rate-to-pricing framework, retroactive avatar definition, and a genuinely clever front-end restructure that turns a $300 DIY product into paid sales calls ("client-financed acquisition"). **Segment B** is Julian Lin's lecture "How to 100x Your Content and Ads" — the single most tactically important segment of the entire workshop for our purposes, because it is a click-by-click walkthrough of the exact Claude Code clipping automation that took the More Mozi channel from 3 clips/day to 35 clips/day at $2.59 per video, plus the full method for building your own automations. This is the blueprint for our ad-asset-creation project. **Segment C** is the VIP+ offer presentation, which — sales pitch aside — contains useful operational models: analyzing 7,300 feedback surveys with Claude, auto-routing support questions, onboarding data portals, and a live demonstration of the firm eating its own pricing/friction dog food.

## Segment A: The Real Estate Hot Seat

### The business

- Family-run **flat-fee MLS listing business** in Ohio. Four family members in the business, all full-time, no outside income ("we are all just this").
- Two price points at the time of the hot seat: a full-service flat-fee listing (~$1,500 pre-hot-seat) and a **$300 DIY package** ("we literally just plug in their listing information to the MLS and walk away").
- ~50 listings/month total, roughly half of which (~25/month) are the $300 DIY product.
- **No paid ads** ("we can't afford it yet"). Funnel is organic search + organic social → homepage → hopefully books a call, or better, checks out entirely self-serve.
- Founder wants **$100K/year take-home** personally; she's currently well short of it.
- Family alignment self-scored 8/10 — the diagnosis was "too many good ideas," not misalignment. Motivation: her 10/10, brother growing into a 9 (previously a 7), newest brother ~5 (employee, "a baby in the market"), stepdad does all the sales calls.
- Best idea that never got executed: **defining the avatar** — "last year, nobody actually did."

### Prescription 1 — Real CAC includes replacing yourself

The opening correction, and it applies to every founder-delivered service business:

> "A lot of you guys are still in delivery for your client services, and you're like, 'Oh, my cost to acquire is nothing.' Well, yeah — but if you replaced yourself, you'd have to hire somebody for a salary that is the market price. And then that divided by new customers is actually the cost to acquire."

"I'm at twenty percent net margin doing great" is an illusion when the founder's labor is priced at zero. Her honest version of the math: her salary + marketing software, divided by customers — that was the real CAC all along. **Rule: price the founder's replacement at market salary and load it into CAC before declaring any channel "free."**

### Prescription 2 — The flat-fee danger

Opening line of the whole session: **"The thing with flat fees is that your costs are not flat, based on the deal."** Their subscriptions are flat yearly, but *time per deal* is not — and that's exactly what they were "struggling with more this year than ever." A flat fee with variable delivery cost means your worst clients quietly consume the margin your best clients generate.

### Prescription 3 — Back into price from the income target

The sequence, verbatim in spirit:

1. "What is the income that, if you were making from this business, you should be super excited about?" → **$100K take-home.**
2. "Do we have to double to get there? What do we have to do to get there?" → **Double.**
3. "So we double the price." → **"Sold! $3,000 it is."**

Price is set backwards from the owner's income target, not forwards from cost-plus or competitor-matching. Then the close-rate framework (below) is used to sanity-check whether the doubled price is *still* too low.

### Prescription 4 — The close-rate → pricing framework

Her stepdad closes **74% of prospects who get on the phone**. The framework:

| Observed close rate | Diagnosis | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **≥ 75%** | Severely mispriced — "you should not close everybody" | **3x the price** |
| **~60% consistently, with everything dialed** (VSL perfect, sales motion, scripting flawless) | Still underpriced | **Raise price 50%** |
| **~40%** | "That's the right closure rate" — the perfect balance of yeses and no's | Price is correct |

The emotional cost is named explicitly: at a correct price, **60% say no, "and emotionally that's hard, but financially it makes a lot of sense."** Applied live: they'd just doubled to $3K; given the 74% close rate, the coach said he'd push to **$4K minimum, probably $5K** ("If I was told at acquisition, I'd probably say, hey, pay five K for the thing"). She admitted the stepdad would be far more open to raising the price "if I tell him that you guys told me to do it."

### Prescription 5 — Find the avatar retroactively

Since nobody defined the avatar prospectively, do it backwards: **"Do a retroactive analysis. Just look at the best customers."** The signals of a best customer, verbatim:

- They **don't complain**
- They **pay right away**
- They **don't have eleven installments for three grand**

For them the discriminating variable was **median home value** — and she already knew the answer once asked: **$350K–$500K sellers in Ohio** are the ideal person. The avatar was latent in the customer data the whole time. Once defined, it becomes a *filter*: "Now when you take calls and leads, you have to filter."

### Prescription 6 — Flow → monetize → then add friction

The staged funnel framework (attributed to Alex: **"He's never made less money by adding more friction to the funnel"**):

1. **Create flow** — get a lot of leads when starting out.
2. **Monetize flow** — sell everybody.
3. **Then add friction** — filters, qualification, pricing gates — *at the end*, once lead volume exceeds capacity.

Explicit warning: don't copy Acquisition.com's late-stage friction (their VSLs, their $5K–$15K workshop tickets) when you're still at the flow stage — "we added friction when we had way too many leads." For this business, *now* was the right point: they had just turned away their **first client ever** this year, which the coach called "a big shift in the business." (They had previously already fired unprofitable *referrers* — the $100-a-listing arrangements that "just wasn't a pay.")

### Prescription 7 — The big unlock: DIY $300 → "done WITH you" $300 = paid sales calls

This is the crown jewel of the hot seat. Current state: ~25/month buy the $300 DIY package (plug listing into MLS, walk away), fully self-serve checkout, zero sales conversation. The restructure:

1. **Re-craft the $300 product as the front end** of a front-end/back-end offer pair.
2. Change it from "done for you (lite)" to **"done WITH you" — "we change one letter"** — with a **VSL on the page** that explains it.
3. The $300 purchase now *includes a call*. "Now you get **three hundred dollars to have a sales call**."
4. On that call, upsell to the full-service package — because **"the best upsell is more of the thing they just bought, or more help with the thing they just bought."**
5. Instead of free ($0) sales calls, every sales conversation is now a **$300 paid sales call** — which means **"we can actually run paid traffic."**

The self-liquidating ad math, verbatim: **"If we can just get one hundred and fifty dollars in ad spend to get us three hundred dollars back, we're profitable on that. And now we can have unlimited sales."** As long as cost-per-front-end-sale ≤ front-end price, the front end pays for the advertising and every back-end sale is acquired at zero (or negative) net cost. He names it: **"It's called client-financed acquisition."** The remaining problems become scaling problems — "wonderfully new problems about people and scaling infrastructure. But they have more zeros on them, so it feels better."

Supporting fix — **reframe the onboarding call**: they had tried offering DIY buyers an "onboarding call" (having noticed post-listing confusion) and almost nobody booked it, because "I am doing it on my own, I am good" — the DIY buyer's identity rejects the frame. The phrase has to change so the call reads as part of the value, not remedial hand-holding. (The transcript cuts off mid-reframe, but the principle is stated: "Reframe" — the call must be positioned as the thing they *bought*, not a support add-on.)

The segment closes with the mentor story — "we just don't know what's possible… you didn't know it was possible to sell a sales call, and now you know, and now the world will change" — and a full VIP scholarship granted to the founder.

## Segment B: Julian Lin — How to 100x Your Content and Ads

### Who Julian is

- **Creative producer and head of content** at Acquisition.com; leads content across all of Alex's platforms.
- Last 12 months: **3 billion impressions, 4 million net new followers, ~1,000 pieces of content produced per week** just for Acquisition.com.
- Before that: built the **#1 learning-science YouTube channel to 2.17M subscribers**; worked with teams behind **MrBeast, Red Bull, and the NFL**. (From Australia, despite the "China" running gag.)

### The More Mozi case study — the numbers that justify the whole project

The second YouTube channel ("More Mozi," ~100K subs at time of talk) is the proof-of-concept for AI-driven clipping volume:

| Phase | Output | How |
|---|---|---|
| ~1 year ago | **3 clips/day** | One full-time editor dedicated to the channel, Monday–Sunday |
| Then | **0 clips/day** | They *stopped* — one editor wasn't enough bandwidth; 3 videos every single day was unsustainable |
| Start of 2026 | **10 clips/day** | One editor + **one automation** — views skyrocketed |
| By July 2026 | **35 clips/day** | Better AI tools + an **AI-first culture** |

The economics comparison:

| | Main channel | More Mozi (second channel) |
|---|---|---|
| Posting cadence | ~1 video/week | **~250 videos/week** |
| Cost per video | **$10,000** | **$2.59** |
| Views | Baseline | ~**half** the monthly views of main |
| **Sales generated** | X | **Same X — equal sales to the main channel** |

The kicker is the last row: tracking the metric that matters (revenue, not views), the $2.59/video channel produces **the same amount of sales as the $10,000/video channel**, because the clips are specific to the right outcome. And "all of this is one automation" — which he then gave away.

Community proof points: Maddox helped develop the automation; Evie made her first vibe-edited short **in Hungarian** (the automation tailors to any content/language); in a pre-automation volume competition inside the community, **Dorina posted 570 times/week**, Aymen 570, Kyle 400; other owners 2x'ed output (e.g., Rodolfo, 7 → 14/week) using just the principles, before the automation.

### The three trends recap (why volume)

All three trends from earlier in the workshop converge on the same behavior change:

1. **Interest media is alive, social media is dead.** Virality doesn't matter; **content is targeting**; target **a hundred different ponds rather than one ocean** (this is exactly what the highlights channel does). → Make more content.
2. **Creative is ephemeral.** It lasts a very short time; you need more targeted callouts; **"the market is not saturated, the creative is."** → Make more content.
3. **Organic and paid content are merging.** High-performing organic becomes your top ads. More organic → more ads → more revenue. → Make more content.

Conclusion: **"We need to create a violent volume and amount of content — both quality and volume."** The obstacle: nobody wants to hire a 10-person editing team or two agencies; hiring takes time, editors are expensive, training takes even more time. The resolution is not hiring — it's that most businesses aren't using AI to its potential, because AI feels amorphous. Guiding quote (Alex): **"Perceived complexity exists in the vague, not the specific."** At Acquisition.com the editors, strategists, and producers all use AI daily — not just engineers — because the amorphous thing called "AI" has been broken down into exact steps and actions. Related Alex framing: *"If you're using AI but you're not making more money, you're using AI wrong."*

### "Vibe editing" — the definition

**Vibe coding** = building software by describing what you want in plain English instead of writing code line by line. **Vibe editing** (their internally coined term) = the same process for editing: instead of opening CapCut / DaVinci / Premiere, you speak to Claude — *"Hey Claude, can you just edit this video in this style?"* — and it does the edit.

### The QR gift bundle

The QR code at the back of the marketing workbooks contains three things:

1. **The Vibe Editing Playbook** — step-by-step, including what to expect at each stage of setup.
2. **The AI editing tool list** — the tools their editors use day-to-day to produce 1,000 pieces/week across all the brands.
3. **A summary of all tools and workflows in the presentation** (so you don't need to memorize the code shown on screen).

### Why business owners should watch the click-by-click even if they never code

> "Even if you're not the one coding — it's your team coding or vibe coding — knowing click by click what happens is still a useful activity because **it allows you to gauge what a reasonable output from your team is.**"

His anecdote: a business owner was paying a marketing agency **$10K/month to make three clips**. After seeing the automation ($2.59/video, 35/day), "let's just say it wasn't a very fun conversation they had with the agency." The point of the walkthrough is to **recalibrate your sense of volume** and of the amount of work a team can do with the right tools.

### THE CLIPPING AUTOMATION — click by click

**Step 1 — Install Claude Code.** An AI coding agent: instead of coding line by line, you tell it what you're building and it builds it. Key properties: it can **access your folders, create folders, and has access to a lot of tools** — critical, because editing has to be broken down into specific actions and behaviors, and actions need tools. The workhorse tool is **ffmpeg** — "this Swiss army knife" that can **trim audio, cut video, render, transcribe**, and handle captions; Claude Code downloads tools like this for you as needed. (Any coding agent works; the fundamentals are the same.)

**Step 2 — Paste the setup prompt (from the QR code).** What the prompt does at a high level:

- First sentence sets up the project (works even for non-technical users; Claude walks through everything step by step).
- It **pulls the codebase from GitHub** — "GitHub is essentially like Google Drive for sharing code." Rather than a black-box tool, you get the actual lines of code behind the automation, which you can inspect and ask Claude to change. "The prompt is the least impressive thing about this — the actual goal is inside that GitHub project."
- It asks for permissions (approvable/skippable).

**Step 3 — The brand interview.** The automation then asks a series of questions about *your* content:

- **Brand name** (he typed "Alex")
- **Colors used**
- **Design conventions**
- **What type of subtitles you want**

This tailors the automation to your specific content and goals — "not a one-size-fits-all model like a lot of the online clipping tools." The playbook covers what to expect at each stage if you get lost.

**Step 4 — Clip (vibe editing in action).** His example prompt, near-verbatim:

> "Can you find five to ten minute clips from this footage that I'm giving you? Each clip should feature one caller. Begin the clip as soon as the business owner introduces their business, and end once the host wraps up."

Then **drag the video file into Claude** — that's it. He dragged in a **90-minute hotline video**.

**What it does under the hood:** identifies all the speakers (who is the host, who is the business owner) using the tools; **reads the transcript**; **understands the context using Claude itself**; **makes the cuts**; **renders the final video with ffmpeg** — and outputs a batch of clipped video files onto your desktop. One drag-and-drop produced **seven clips**; "if I just did that a couple more times, that's thirty-five" — the secret behind 35 posts/day.

**Prompt controls the edit point:** the clip started where it did *because the prompt said* "start when the business owner introduces themselves." Want clips to start at a point of tension instead? Change the prompt.

### The 7-step build-your-own-automation method

He then rebuilds the More Mozi automation from scratch to teach the method ("I don't just want to give you a fish, I want to teach you how to fish" — essential because new AI tools ship every month and the fundamentals outlast any one tool).

**1. Preparation — automate the right thing.** You don't want to be "the person that uses AI to make bad content faster." Checklist before any automation:

- **Education vs entertainment**: automating entertainment content probably won't lead to sales.
- **Name the hook**: naming hooks helps content perform, *and* it lets you **train the AI to identify your hooks** — "when you script out a good hook, or you tell AI the types of hooks to look out for, it's going to do a much better job at clipping."
- **Specific avatar**: be the biggest fish in a small pond; your name should be associated with the niche.
- **Production**: gear doesn't matter much — clean audio and convenience do.
- **Effective production beats overproduction**: for every graphic, text, or sound effect, ask "is this helping the audience understand the video more?" If not, it's overproduction. Sound effects, crazy music tracks, booms and whooshes — "probably not needed for education content that converts." One of the highest-performing formats on Alex's channel right now is a raw, authentic, GoPro-style talking video. **Education video needs little editing — which makes it easier to automate.**

**2. Reverse prompting.** Regular prompting: "Claude, do X." Reverse prompting: **"I have this goal to make this automation. How would you go about this? What are all the tools available to do this? Can you map out a step-by-step roadmap?"** This first tells you whether the automation is even *feasible*, then gives you a concrete plan. His actual seed prompt: "I want to create a video editor that takes a video file, clips five-to-ten-minute clips from it, outputs those video files — and asks me clarifying questions to help me plan out this project."

**3. Plan mode before send.** Claude Code has modes (bottom-left toggle): accept edits, **plan mode**, auto mode. Start in plan mode. The analogy: building a house, you lay out the whole framework first — you don't frame the bedroom, paint the bedroom, furnish the bedroom, and then start the kitchen from scratch. Map the high-level frame, *then* let Claude execute. Move to accept-edits or auto mode "depending on how much trust you have in Claude."

**4. Ask Claude to ask *you*.** Because the prompt included "ask me clarifying questions," Claude pops up questions before building. "Think of it as training a new employee — you need to give it a lot of context, and sometimes you don't know the exact context it needs, which is why you can ask Claude to prompt you on what context it needs." If unsure of an answer, ask Claude to recommend based on what you want.

**5. The three clip-selection approaches** (Claude's own clarifying question: "how should I decide which parts of the video become clips?" — i.e., what's the *brain* of the editor):

| Approach | How it works | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed time-based chunks** | Cut every N minutes | Not ideal — "it's not as if every five minutes there's going to be a clip"; cuts land weird |
| **Scene / visual detection** | Cut on scene changes | Fits *entertainment* content, where a scene change is often a good cut |
| **Whisper transcription + LLM segment-finding** | Transcribe audio with **Whisper** (the OpenAI open model that turns MP3/audio into text), then use an **LLM (Claude) to find segments** matching a prompt | **The standard for education creators** — this is where the prompt matters |

**Prompt engineering the LLM layer.** Base example: *"Find clips where they discuss pricing."* Then add layers: *make sure the hook says this; make sure it covers these three tension points; make sure the payoff is strong* — and **give it examples**. To go a step further: **find clips online that perform well and prompt "extract the first principles that made this clip successful" — that extraction then becomes your clipping prompt.** Mids vs shorts: mid-form is just clipping a start and end; **shorts need a more sophisticated prompt** because you must tell Claude exactly what to include and exclude.

Remaining clarifying questions: what content type you'll feed it (his: talking-head), and how you want to run it — **command-line interface** (more technical) or, what he personally uses, **a simple UI: a small web page you open locally** to navigate the automation more easily.

**6. Review the plan.** After the Q&A, Claude produces two panes: left = its history of what it did; right = **the plan** — "the step-by-step instruction manual Claude will use to execute and build your invention." You do *not* need to understand every line — but **the context paragraph at the top is the most important paragraph to read and proofread**: make sure it describes what you actually need. Revise as needed, then hit **accept / auto mode** to execute. A few minutes later: a local UI pops up — choose a video file, describe what the clip should contain, run. Want a new feature ("I want to run multiple videos at the same time")? Just ask Claude to add it. His demo automation (the Pokémon clip of Alex) was **built in ~10 minutes, fully by Claude**.

**7. Iterate.** The loop: **be crystal clear on what you're building → go back and forth on the plan (this is where the majority of *your* time is spent) → execute → adjust and repeat.** Bonus study technique: feed the More Mozi automation code (in the QR) back to Claude and ask **"Explain how this automation was built, step by step"** — open the hood and understand exactly what happens.

### The extended tool list (rapid fire)

- **GitHub** — keep code safe, track progress, store versions.
- **Claude Skills** — "**SOPs for AI**." If you explain the same concept to Claude repeatedly and it never quite gets it, package it as a skill: a repeatable prompt sent every time.
- **App connections** — connect the automation to Slack / Gmail / Calendar (e.g., get pinged when a render finishes); ask Claude how, or use the Claude desktop app.
- **shadcn** — open-source UI components (used by OpenAI) to make your automation's UI good.
- **Deploy + auth** — put the tool on a website; add authentication if you deploy.
- **Supabase** — database for your program. Powering example: **Alex's Threads account is end-to-end automated with no human in the loop** — it posts every hour, pulling tweets stored in Supabase.
- **Cron jobs** — tasks on a recurring schedule; this is what removes the human. The Threads automation **runs daily around 4 AM, pulls ~24 tweets, and schedules them onto Threads.** Several ways to set one up; just ask Claude.
- **Sub-agents** — Claude spawning smaller workers to build code in parallel, much faster.
- **Whisper Flow** — speak, it transcribes automatically; used heavily internally (dictate your prompts).
- **Apify** — his stated favorite: a catalog of **pre-built tools** (scrapers, integrations). Instead of vibe-coding a Twitter/X scraper from scratch (moving data from one platform to another takes real time to build), tell Claude: "this tool exists — call it and integrate it into my automation." This is how the Threads pipeline gets Alex's tweets into the database.

### The full chained content pipeline (~1,000 posts/week)

Automations chain into workflows. The YouTube pipeline for Alex's content, box by box:

1. **Scrape tweets** (automation, via Apify) — Alex's tweets are the raw script material
2. **Arrange tweets into themes** (automation)
3. **Turn themes into scripts** (automation)
4. **Alex records** ← the only human box
5. **Detect new footage** (automation)
6. **Editing workflow** (the clipping automation) → outputs mid-form
7. **Schedule and post** (automation)
8. **Pull performance data** (automation) → informs which tweets to pull next time — the loop closes

Every box except "Alex records" is automated. And that entire chain is **one platform** — it's replicated for Instagram, LinkedIn, TikTok, Facebook, Pinterest, X, and Reddit. **"This is how we're able to post a thousand times a week."** The same chaining pattern applies beyond marketing: sales, CS, HR, recruiting, finance.

Closing lines: **"Doing more is still the highest risk-adjusted return move in the age of AI,"** and the Alex quote: **"You really want to do so much volume, it would be unreasonable for you to fail."**

## Segment C: The Offer Presentation

Kept brief — the pitch itself isn't our concern, but several operational models in it are worth stealing.

- **Survey analysis at scale with Claude.** They analyzed **7,300+ feedback surveys collected over 29 months** by running them through Claude ("Claude, analyze this"). Findings: of ~700 constructive comments, **~34% (≈2,500 respondents overall) wanted more personalized help** and **~20% wanted more advisor access** — i.e., roughly 1 in 3 wanted more personalized support, 1 in 5 more advisor access. The VIP+ offer was *built from* that analysis. Model: your feedback/reviews/intake notes are a product-design dataset; an LLM can mine them in an afternoon.
- **Support mechanics worth copying.** (1) Hard **24-hour response guarantee** ("usually way faster"). (2) Questions **auto-ping to Slack** — no scrolling a community feed — and the system **automatically identifies and tags the best-suited person** because every advisor has specialty categories. (3) **Onboarding data portal**: a mandatory ~1-hour onboarding where clients upload their data; advisors then answer questions against a "living snapshot" of the business **without ten rounds of clarifying back-and-forth**. The stated principle: **"Good decisions require good data."** They note they couldn't do this six months earlier — it's AI-enabled. Team: 64 members in the Vantage community, 22 full-time dedicated advisors.
- **Pricing architecture and friction dogfooding.** VIP+ is **$36K/year**; entry mechanism is a **$2,500 deposit plus a one-on-one onboarding call** (friction at the point of sale, exactly per their own framework). Workshop tickets repriced two weeks earlier on supply-and-demand logic: **first workshop $5K, second-and-beyond $15K**, because half of every room is now returning clients — and 50% of seats are reserved for clients (VIPs attend two intensives/year free). "We follow our own advice." Deal framing: only **0.02% of business owners** (2.6 per 10,000) ever do a private equity deal; their decade goal is 10,000+ owners helped and 2–4 multi-billion-dollar exits.
- **Named bonuses (relevant to us as content, not as pitch).** Alex's **pricing playbook** — 10 pricing plays (price guarantee, fee-form play, long-term billing, "ultra pricing," CPI escalators, +5 more) — plus all 12 post-launch playbooks (branding, fast cash, proof, marketing machine, lifetime value, price raises). A **Meta Ads Paid Crash Course**: a recorded walkthrough including **"how to win with Andromeda"** (Meta's new ranking/creative-modeling stack — directly relevant to our $5–10K/month Meta plans) and **"how to make the 50 hooks in 5 minutes"** — Drew (head of AI) trainings that ship *with prompts*: watch a 10-minute video, take the prompt into your AI with your portal data, get 50 ad hooks for your business. And a **VSL Masterclass** (Alex, 60 minutes): step-by-step VSL scripting using AI, **where to place VSLs (before and after thank-you pages)**, and per-industry nuance (e.g., home services plays it on an iPad in the house). "Who has a VSL right now? … It should be everybody. It's the easiest lift of all time."
