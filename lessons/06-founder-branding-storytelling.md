# Founder Branding & Storytelling for Growth (Dion — Acquisition.com Workshop, 08/20)

Dion (former Disney boy-band lead singer turned brand agency founder, sold the agency in 2024, now at Acquisition.com) taught a session on founder branding and storytelling as a business-growth lever — "content and how to leverage it to actually grow the business and attract great talent instead of the useless shit that we see floating around." His core argument: your mission, values, and identity must be stated up front (not buried on an About page) because they act as a "bat signal" to the right customers and the right A-player talent; humans judge the whole company by every part they touch (the "unconscious parallel assumption"), so every touchpoint must be congruent; and content only counts when it's measured by commercial signals — clicks, calls, sales, buying questions in DMs — not likes and follows. The lecture ran about 14 minutes, followed by open Q&A with a transportation/logistics company, a law-firm brand director, a dental group founder, a marriage-coaching business, and a husband-wife co-founder pair.

---

## Lessons & Frameworks

### 1. Lead with mission, values, and identity — send the "bat signal" first

Dion's opening diagnosis: "I'll go to somebody's website in this room and I'll have to search for the mission statement somewhere on a page, like 'About Us' ... But you don't lead with it. And when you don't lead with it, oftentimes we kind of like, 'Oh well, we're going to be a little more general.' We start to sand off our edges so that we can attract more people. ... And what is happening is you miss the opportunity to send that bat signal to the right humans."

Specifics:
- Talk about "your mission and your values and who you are, and your hell-yeses and your hell-nos, and where you are going, what you are really about — it is so much easier to find the right people. But you got to do it in the front."
- This is as much a **talent** strategy as a customer strategy: "You need the best talent to come in ... The best of best has options; they're not going to come for money; they're going to come for value alignment. Something to be proud of ... You all have to be humans and founders that someone would want to follow into the battle."
- Dion's own hiring story is the proof case: he watched Alex Hormozi's content ("a guy with a beard, he looked like a lumberjack ... within three seconds he's talking to me"), decided "I could work for someone for the first time in my life," and got hired out of 120+ applicants — because the founder's content told the truth about the mission and the culture. "If we don't know who you are, then you're just a logo, you're just a company."
- Differentiation and defensibility: "What differentiates you is each of you. You guys are unique as your DNA ... You cannot be copied if you talk about the things that make you unique." Founder brand is a "defensible moat" against commoditization.
- If the founder refuses to be the face, an affiliate/influencer/deeply-bought-in team member can front the brand — but see the warning below.

### 2. The unconscious parallel assumption — pizza joint, influencer collapse, and brand guidelines

**The principle:** "We have this thing as humans ... called the unconscious parallel assumption. It's basically just that humans will judge the whole by the parts."

**The pizza-joint metaphor:** "Let's say we're all on a party bus ... and we see a pizza joint, and it's like 'best pizza in the world' but the parking lot is empty, there is graffiti on the wall, there is trash blowing around. We assume that the product behind it is shit, right? ... Conversely, parking lot is full. There's a line at the door. There's music playing. ... Probably a good product. The first one might be a better product, but humans will unconsciously judge the entire operation based on those pieces." Takeaway: "If you guys aren't being intentional about the associations and the pairings that you're causing in the marketplace, the humans are going to do it for you."

**The influencer 60%-revenue collapse:** A past attendee's e-com company did a deal with an influencer who "represented about sixty percent of their revenue ... crushing it. But she posted one thing about Charlie Kirk — her personal view — and it was so politically charged that the market associated that comment and her belief with the company. They assumed it was the same belief, and it went to almost zero — their revenue — because of that association." The brand is a company asset — "that brand is IP, and if you have the wrong person, they can actually hurt you."

**The faith-based org / twerking example:** Another company said "We believe in God, we are a faith-based organization, this is how we talk about ourselves" — then let an unvetted person touch the brand who "was popping bottles and twerking at the club." Direct contradiction of the stated identity, transferred straight onto the company.

**The prescription — written brand guidelines:** "You have to make sure that you've got clear guidelines on who you are. Here is how we talk about ourselves. Here are the things we never say." Anyone who fronts or touches the brand must be vetted and must operate inside those guidelines, because everything on their channel "will stick to the company."

### 3. Touchpoint congruence audit — the Lorem Ipsum thank-you-page story

A client in troubled-teen mentoring had a beautiful funnel — "beautiful story, beautiful video sales letter" — but "we get all these bookings and then this huge drop-off on show-up rate. We can't figure out why." Dion audited the entire journey and found the thank-you page **after booking** still had Lorem Ipsum placeholder copy. "Imagine a parent who's emotional and freaked out about sending their kid somewhere ... nobody audited their stuff."

Key mechanics of the lesson:
- The prospect doesn't consciously reason about it: "They're not going to call you and say, 'Hey, your thank-you page made me feel different than the other things I saw about you.' They're just going to go away."
- Incongruence shows up as mystery metrics: "Sometimes it's a mystery on what the hell is happening with your close rate or churn — like why is that happening? Make sure you audit all of it."
- Audit **every** touchpoint from ad → landing page → booking → confirmation → reminders → first contact, looking for any point where the message, tone, or quality breaks from the rest.

### 4. The anonymous internal brand survey

Dion's take-home exercise: "When you get home, send out a survey — anonymous, because people will try to impress the boss and say what they think you want to hear. Make it anonymous and say, 'I'd just love to know how you see us. What do you think our values are? What do you think our mission is? Who we serve? What are we about? Why do we do it?'"

Why it matters: "Imagine sales talks about you one way, customer success talks about you a slightly different way, your social media team sees you another way. Imagine what's happening in that process when the customer or talent is going through those different parts ... There's a trust gap that happens if it's not congruent and unified." The fix sequence: clarify the identity first ("get it super crystal clear"), then hand everyone the same playbook — "here's how we talk about ourselves; we don't ever say these things."

### 5. Message the outcome, not the category — the stay-at-home-mom demo

Dion role-played the buyer's actual moment: a stay-at-home mom mid-chaos — toddler with no diaper, another kid "trying to plug a fork in the outlet," the shower running forgotten for eight minutes — who finally sits down and pulls up her phone. The algorithm knows her life and serves her content.

- Weak (category) message: "Hey, if you are a stay-at-home mom, I help you make extra money."
- Strong (outcome) message: "I help stay-at-home moms get some me time, a little bit of peace, time to take a fucking shower or have girl time or pursue their favorite hobbies — and still be an amazing mom. Ooh, now you're talking."

The principle: "If you think about your business in the outcome that people get, the result they get from working with you — now we're getting more human. Now we're talking about something where it matters." And the economics behind it: "That delta between people who charge premium and the commodity price — that's all brand. We've got to get people to care about your company beyond pricing ... Otherwise, I am shopping for the best deal." His illustrations: paying double for coffee at a family-run West Hollywood café because "they talk about things that matter to me — I am their ideal person," and a $50 shirt becoming $650 once a logo is added — "same damn fabric," the premium is entirely the associations the brand built with its ideal people.

### 6. Tell the truth about who you are — the $50M founder

A past attendee doing $50M/year raised his hand: "I fucking hate people ... I hate most of my team and I hate most of my customers ... I feel like I have to wear a mask for different scenarios and it's exhausting, and it makes me hate my business." He was an "edgy Jersey guy" performing sensitivity at the office and formality with clients. Dion asked how he got his **best** customer: "I was at an event and we hit it off ... We hung out at the bar. We had some beer." They bonded as themselves.

The prescription: "Imagine if you were that guy on the front end. So you told the truth about who you are as a founder and the culture of your business from the very beginning — who do you think you'd attract more of?" To the fear of turning people off: "Yeah, but then you are building a business you just told me you hate." The close: "Talk about it. Be who you are. Tell the truth. State the facts. You are awesome the way you are, and your people — the best people — are going to love you." Supporting example: if Hormozi "came down here in a suit and tie right now ... it'd be really weird. I would not have worked here if I got to the office and he was somebody else. He's exactly who he is — the content — and so is he; they are the same."

### 7. Content cadence rules

From the transportation/logistics Q&A, but stated as universal rules ("this is for everybody"):

- **Sustainable cadence for a year:** "You want to do content at a cadence at which you can keep up without stopping. So if you're like, 'I'm busy, I can only do this two or three times a week' ... but you can do that consistently without breaking for a year — I'd rather you do that than the hero move, where you try a bunch of shit for a couple weeks, then you get busy and stop again for a month. You need the momentum, you need the reps to get a decent sample size to actually make the best next decision."
- **Volume negates luck:** More people posting and more attempts = "the more violent you can get with the amount of volume ... the faster you are going to get meaningful data to make the best decision." Multiple founders posting is "multiple looks at the basket" — not necessary, but it accelerates finding what works.
- **Document, don't create:** "Double dip by documenting what you already do. If you're walking a job site, if you're on a call with a vendor or a client, if you're onboarding somebody — record it." Redact/mute client details where needed ("make their voice sound like a fucking robot"), but show "how you roll in your process." No separate production day required — "it could just be set up the camera before you [start]."
- **Commercial metrics, not vanity:** "Not popular as likes, views, follows — all that shit. I am looking for what got the click, what got the call, what got the sale, what got the buying questions in DMs. I am looking for those signals. Then we want to double down on that content — whoever it was, doesn't matter."

### 8. People follow people, not logos

From the husband-wife co-founder Q&A: "How many people — quick show of hands — woke up this morning and said, 'I can't wait to see what Acquisition.com posted on Instagram'? Nobody. Because people don't care about companies; they care about the humans. We go on social to follow our favorite people ... So it's got to be human-driven." Corollary: any spokesperson or additional avatar-facing channel must be a **personal brand channel** that "all points to the same front door" — the company page can be cross-promoted, but the following attaches to a human. And "everything else on their channel" must stay controlled, "because that will stick to the company."

## Q&A / Room Exchanges

### (a) Transportation/logistics — should all three co-founders make content?

Three co-founders (Nick, Tyler, and the asker); the asker had stepped out of content because he closes deals. Question: does it matter whether it's one of them or all three?

Dion's full prescription:
- "I want content and brand to be a thing that drives business growth, period." Use "the boy-band effect in the beginning, where there are different characters telling the same story, pointing to the same front door" — then figure out which "band member" is driving **business**, "not popular as likes, views, follows, all that shit. I am looking for what got the click, what got the call, what got the sale, what got the buying questions in DMs."
- Multiple people posting is "multiple looks at the basket — you are just increasing the likelihood that you are going to find that artifact faster ... but it is not necessary. It's the volume-negates-luck thing."
- When they said Nick drove the most engagement but Tyler ran marketing, and they didn't have enough data to know which content drives business, Dion delivered the cadence rules (sustainable cadence for a year over the "hero move"; document-don't-create by recording job-site walks, vendor calls, onboarding, with client info redacted; plus intentional pieces layered on top).

### (b) Law-firm brand director — separate website for the personal-injury attorney?

The asker: "I work as a brand director at a law firm ... trying to build the personal injury [practice] — one attorney that specializes in that and is growing his personal business ... he is the face; he does all the speaking. Is it okay to do a separate website?"

Dion's full prescription:
- "We have found that it is way more profitable if you have **specific landing pages** and a landing-page experience **for the avatar you are calling out in the content**." The failure mode: "if we say, 'Hey, if you are a plumber ... click here' and you click the link, and it takes you to a page that just talks about business in general — you might lose some folks. **I want them to feel seen all the way through the process, as long as you can.**"
- Build "a specific landing-page experience that drives very specific behavior with **not a bunch of exit points**." Concretely for the attorney: "When they click on the brain-injury case they want somebody to look at, they land on the page, and it's **him**, and it's **a video that's talking just to that avatar**, driving into a call-booking opportunity and a consultation for that avatar. That's going to maximize your opportunity for conversion."
- On URL structure: "Is that landing page still within that same URL?" — "Absolutely. Or it could be a subdomain; **no one cares about that. No one's looking at 'wait a minute, this URL is different — you suck.'** Did you give them valuable information on the front end? Was your attorney useful and trusted and knowledgeable and helpful? And then when I went through the process, did he continue the conversation?" (Caveat noted: "there's other conversations to be had about SEO value and stuff, but that's a different thing.")

### (c) Dental group — will people only want to see the co-founder, or can trust transfer to the brand?

Artic, a dentist with a dental group: the group brand page is only followed by "dental people"; he hasn't done personal branding "partially because I don't want people to want to see **me** — I want them to see the eighteen other dentists we have, because I don't see patients every day. Is that a false assumption?"

Dion's full prescription:
- "It depends on how you talk about it. Obviously, if you become a trusted, known, loved person in the space, people are going to be like, 'Hey, how do I see **you**?' So it's how you position it."
- The Hormozi model: "You'll notice that Alex always wears the acquisition.com stuff everywhere, on the biggest podcasts, everywhere he goes. And when he talks about it, you'll notice language like **'we' and 'us' and 'my team' and 'my advisors.'** ... What it does is it **transfers that brand association and trust to the 'we' — to the entity — and to your people.**"
- "You can also do **spotlights on people**, and they can also make content. So you are hitting it from multiple directions, **building affinity from multiple humans, all pointing to the same practice.**" The founder's personal brand becomes a trust-funnel into the whole team, not a bottleneck onto one provider.

### (d) Marriage-coaching business — "do not pivot on assumptions"

The company helps men turn around marriages and avoid divorce. The co-founder's business partner (a licensed marriage & family therapist) makes content aimed at "nice guys and emotional men"; the co-founder, head of sales, and CEO are "more logical people" and the content "doesn't resonate" with them. Competitors entered the space speaking to a "logical" avatar; the business slid; call volume shifted from entrepreneurs/business owners (2–3 years ago) to mostly blue-collar workers. They were considering hiring a logical-style spokesperson. Should that funnel be a different brand?

Dion's full prescription:
- **Same brand.** "However, I have a question: what data do you have to prove that the content he makes versus the content you want to make is not effective at driving business growth?"
- **Call out the avatar in every piece:** "If you are not getting the kind of clients that you want, then it's because you are not talking to them and being valuable to them. You need to explicitly call out who those people are ... if there are different types inside there, you can call them out — each content piece you put out, you can call out every individual version of that."
- **Do not pivot on assumptions:** "I wouldn't move forward on assumptions that aren't based on data. You have to prove it before you start making big pivots to how you communicate. **Look at your best clients. Look at the last sixty–ninety days of the people that came through the front door and paid to work with you guys.** Figure out who they are, how they came in, what resonated with them, why they love you, why they stayed, why they're still giving you money every month. Once you can reverse-engineer that, then you can figure out how to bring that data to the front end and talk to those people."
- **Don't chase competitors:** "I don't want you running around pivoting your whole thing to chase a competitor, because they're not you. You guys are you."
- **Team opinions are not data:** When they countered that half the team doesn't resonate with the content: "I am less interested in half your team resonating with it. I am more interested in what type of content is getting you the **right kind of phone calls** ... Bill in accounting not resonating with the content is not relevant unless he's your ideal avatar ... unless he's a buyer — like your best buyer — I am not so much interested in his opinion." Also on the assumption that successful men are logical: "There are logical, data-driven successful people. There are emotional, heart-driven successful people. You have to ask yourself: who do you want? Who are your best clients?"
- **Resolve co-founder misalignment with data, not opinion:** "You guys have a misalignment on how you should be doing that — let the data prove it. 'Hey, we both want to grow this business ... let's figure out how to do more of that by using data and not opinion.'"
- **Spokesperson/new avatar:** fine, "it all points to the same front door, but it needs to be a **human channel, a personal-brand channel**" (this is where the "nobody wakes up excited to see what Acquisition.com posted" riff landed) — "and just be careful who it touches ... make sure that everything else on their channel is in control with how you guys want to be paired and associated, because that will stick to the company."

### (e) Husband-wife co-founders — content cannibalization and "turbo bomb the thing that's working"

Question: when the two co-founders are husband and wife both creating content, how do you avoid "content cannibalization"? And if the data shows one person's content drives more booked calls and business opportunities, "should we still try to optimize the other content, or should we just focus and double down on the one that is working?"

Dion: "**Turbo bomb the thing that's working!** All we want is business growth and to reach the people that we want to reach. That's driving the business, period. And if you find a winner, **go hard in the paint on that until you have data to prove that's no longer effective, and then we'll make a new decision.**"

He added a live content critique (to "Rory"): "You are obviously great on stage, great in front of the camera ... The one little tip I would give you is that **I don't often know who you are talking to. I don't often see you call out the person.** If there are multiple avatars underneath — because you can serve a variety of people — hook it with multiple people. 'Hey, if you are a business owner...' ... If you just do generic or general business, you are going to get a whole bunch of general people. But call them out: is there an income level? Is there a certain background? A certain type of person who is your best people? Call them out. I think you would see an improvement in reach to the right folks."
