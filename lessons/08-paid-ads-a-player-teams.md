# Workshop 08: Optimizing Paid Advertising and Building A-Player Teams

**Source:** Acquisition.com / Mozi workshop, 2026-08-20. Two roundtables. Part 1 is ACQ's paid-media lead teaching how they actually run Meta ads under the Andromeda algorithm — mechanics, account structure, qualification, and offer matching — followed by six hot seats. Part 2 is a leadership roundtable on hiring and structuring A-player teams: the interview process, using brand as a talent funnel, the sales org and comp structure, the sales-manager daily rhythm, and org design. This is the single most operationally important transcript for the firm's Meta ads plan: Part 1 is close to a complete standard operating procedure for how to structure a $5–10K/month Meta account, and the Austin dental hot seat is a near-exact template for a multi-location local-services business.

## Part 1: Paid Advertising

### The one sentence that governs everything: "The creative IS the targeting"

The speaker opened with the most important consequence of Andromeda (Meta's new ad-delivery algorithm, discussed the prior day):

> "The most important thing to remember about Andromeda is this one: **the creative is the targeting.**"

### How Andromeda works mechanically

When you upload a creative, this is the sequence:

1. **Meta takes the creative and shows it to a pocket of people — a few hundred people who know nothing about you.**
2. **It tracks who interacts with the ad:** who stops scrolling, who clicks, who watches the whole video (if it's a video).
3. **It then expands: it shows your ad to more people like the ones who interacted with it.**

The failure mode follows directly:

> "If you have a very general and engaging ad, and you get a bunch of people to interact with it, then Meta doesn't know who to show your ad to. And you're going to end up spending a bunch of money to get zero clients."

A broadly appealing ad generates interaction signals from everyone, so the lookalike expansion is aimed at no one. You must be **specific in calling out the avatar and the pain point** so that only your best-fit prospect interacts, and Meta's expansion engine chases that person.

> "Meta is very good at bringing you people. But you need to point it in the right direction, because the whole game in marketing is figuring out how to get more of the good leads while you stop accidentally advertising to the bad ones."

### The roofing worked example (verbatim)

**The bad creative** — a high-end roofing business in Dallas:

> "Are you a homeowner in Dallas? And are you looking to get your roof fixed? We can help you out. Click below or call us."

On paper it checks every box — it calls out the avatar (homeowner in Dallas) and the need (roof fixed). But who clicks on it?

- People with a **$5,000 budget** and people with a **$100,000 budget**
- People who need the roof fixed **tomorrow because a tree fell on their house**, and people who are **price-shopping and might want a new roof next year**
- People who want to **pay cash** and people who want to **use insurance**
- People in **downtown Dallas** and people in **the suburbs**

Not all of those are good leads for a high-end roofer — and the general creative trains Andromeda on all of them indiscriminately.

**The good creative:**

> "Do you own a one-million-dollar-plus house in Highland Park? And every time you get out of your house, you see your neighbor's new roof and you're like, 'Damn, I need to get a new roof because mine is fucked up.' And you're afraid of actually having people over because you don't want them to see your roof."

Why it works:

- It calls out **objective facts about the avatar** — $1M+ home, a specific neighborhood — that predict how the person will behave: they live in a very expensive neighborhood with a very expensive house, so they probably don't care about price, they care about quality. You can propose a very high-end price, sell on quality, and they'll probably buy upsells, memberships, insurance, and anything else you have.
- The pain point **leverages emotion** (embarrassment in front of the neighbors, afraid to have people over) rather than a generic "need roof fixed."

### The four levers

> "We have four things that we need to make sure we are optimizing so that we have efficient and effective paid acquisition: **targeting, creative, account setup, and offer.**"

Note also: **"Ninety-five percent of the marketing and data issues that we see here at ACQ are related to the first three"** — targeting, creative, account setup. Only if those three are verified working and you still have conversion issues do you look at the offer.

#### Lever 1: Targeting — the top-20%-LTV segmentation analysis

> "Most businesses that come here don't know who their best customer is, because who buys your product or service is not necessarily your best customer."

The procedure:

1. **Take the list of all the clients you have ever served.**
2. **Calculate the LTV — the lifetime gross profit — of each client.**
3. **Look at the top twenty percent.**
4. **Ask: what do these customers have in common?**
   - B2B: industry, revenue, employees.
   - B2C: demographics, profession, salary.
5. That tells you **who they are**. Then, for both B2B and B2C, find **what their pain point is — why did they buy your product or service?** If you don't know, **call them**. Have some of your people call. If you serve thousands of people, send them an Amazon gift card and a survey. (Transcript reads "twenty thousand dollars Amazon gift card" — almost certainly a transcription error for a ~$20 gift card incentive per respondent.)

> "It's crucial to know who they are and what their pain point is, because then we go to number two: the creative. We want to call out the avatar."

#### Lever 2: Creative — avatar + pain callouts, and going narrow

- **Call out the avatar and the pain point** — because the creative is the targeting (see the roofing example above).
- ACQ goes extremely narrow, down to **per-industry static images**:

> "Here we go as narrow as having static images, and we call out each individual industry... Are you a commercial real estate [firm]? Are you a landscaping company? Are you marketing leaders? So go super narrow."

- But narrowness lives in the **ad**, not necessarily in the funnel. When the financial-services attendee asked whether each narrow segment (e.g., single moms who want to learn stock trading) needs its own funnel, the answer was no:

> "They all have the same problems, right? So you personalize the **ad**. The landing page is one, because the avatar is one; they might have different things that pull them in... but all of you guys are one avatar: business owners that want to scale, but different industries, different constraints, different revenue levels."

One avatar, one landing page/VSL — many narrow ad-level callouts feeding it.

#### The Golden BB: kill and re-upload to retrain

> "When we upload a creative on Meta, Meta takes the creative and trains it on a pocket of people. **It can happen that Meta trains your creative on the wrong pocket.** So if you think that you have a very good creative and it's not performing, what we suggest is the following: **kill it and re-upload it again in a different ad set. Then it's going to retrain on a different bucket of people. Give it a couple of days.**"

A good creative can fail purely because of an unlucky initial training pocket. Before you conclude a creative is bad, give it a second draw from the deck.

#### Lever 3: Account setup — "test with ABO, scale with CBO"

Two ways to allocate budget inside a campaign: **ABO** (ad set budget optimization — budget set at the ad-set level) and **CBO** (campaign budget optimization — budget set at the campaign level and distributed by Meta).

> "Here at HQ, **we test with ABO and we scale with CBO.**"

The exact ACQ testing workflow:

1. **Take ONE piece of creative** (e.g., "Alex talking on stage about something").
2. **Create 5–10 variations of it:** different music, make it shorter, make it longer, start it a couple of seconds later.
3. **Put all those variations in one ad set** and give budget to that ad set (ABO).
4. **Find the winner** — if there is one.
5. When you find the winner: **increase the budget on that ad set, then duplicate the winner into a CBO campaign.**
6. **Maintain multiple CBO campaigns — one per angle.** When any test ad set produces a winner, the winner is duplicated into the CBO campaign for its respective angle.

**ACQ's angle taxonomy** (each angle = its own CBO scaling campaign):

- Static AI images
- Alex talking on stage
- The Mozi Hall
- Testimonials from the workshops
- Alex talking directly to camera

#### Optimize for a QUALIFIED lead, not a lead

> "Most businesses that we see optimize for leads, and leads are most of the time considered anybody that fills out a form with your contact information."

Instead:

- **Put qualifying questions on the back end of the form: three to five objective questions that you know to be true about your best customer** (derived from the segmentation analysis).
- **Place the Meta pixel/conversion event AFTER the qualification form**, so the algorithm optimizes toward people who qualify — not toward anyone willing to give a name and email.

> "We're not just training Meta to send us traffic that is willing to give us their name and their email — we're actually training it to send us traffic that is qualified."

#### The two-identical-thank-you-pages trick

If you don't want to sacrifice unqualified leads (you'd still like to work them manually):

> "You have a thank-you page. **Duplicate this thank-you page — same page, two different URLs. On one page you put the pixel; on the other page you don't.** Once they fill out the form, the qualified leads go to the page with the pixel; the unqualified go to the second page without the pixel. **You are still getting all of the leads, but you are training your pixel only on qualified ones.**"

An attendee summarized it and the speaker confirmed: "Ideally, you kill the lead you don't want. But if you're not ready or willing to kill the lead that you don't want — you only let the pixel train on the leads you want. Correct."

#### Audience settings: broad + lookalikes + CAPI

- **ACQ does not use audience targeting.** "We use broad audience, because the creative is the targeting."
- **Lookalikes are still used** as a supplement ("We use lookalikes... yeah, combine it") — e.g., seeded from people tagged as qualified in your CRM.
- **Third layer: CAPI (Meta's Conversions API), installed in your CRM.** "That's another way to relay high-quality data back to Meta — so you can say, this lead that you sent me actually **bought** my product, bottom of funnel." Caveats: it's a longer cycle and slower volume (fewer purchasers than leads), "but it's very helpful."

So the full data stack is: **broad audiences + pixel firing on the qualified event only + lookalikes from CRM-tagged best leads + CAPI feeding closed-won purchases back to Meta.**

#### Lever 4: The offer — must match the avatar's expectations

> "The offer needs to be aligned with the expectations of the avatar that you are targeting."

Two failure examples, both explicit:

1. **Underpriced for the avatar:** "If we are targeting homeowners that have homes that are one million plus, and we sell them a full roof for **two thousand dollars** — how many do you think we're gonna sell? **Zero.** They're not gonna trust us. They're gonna think it's a scam, and they're gonna think we're not going to do a good job."
2. **Too big a leap for cold traffic:** "If we were targeting a cold Meta ads campaign to a landing page with self-checkout for a **twenty-thousand-dollar course** — how many do you think we'll sell? **Zero.** People don't have enough time to build trust from a landing page to then buy a $20,000 course. We probably need a **bridge offer** — something cheaper — so we can build trust, and then we can offer them the twenty thousand dollars."

> "The offer, and how you present it, needs to be targeted to the expectations that your audience has."

#### The recap (verbatim structure)

1. Know who your best customer is — who they are and what their pain is — via segmentation analysis.
2. Call them out in your creative — avatar and pain point.
3. Set up the account correctly: test with ABO, scale with CBO, optimize for the qualified event.
4. If those three are clicked, then go look at your offer.

### Creative volume rule

From the marriage-coaching hot seat, the general rule of thumb:

> "**Per ten thousand dollars a month of ad spend, ten new creatives a week.**"

(The attendee spending $200K/month was testing ~100 creatives/month — roughly 25/week — and was told flatly: "You need to test more." At $200K/month the rule implies ~200/week. Out of volume testing, "you're going to understand which one keeps speaking the most to your top clients.")

### Nurture cadence (ACQ's back-end machine)

Asked directly "what is your back-end nurture machine like when a lead does not convert right away," the answer:

> "**The first seven days are critical.** What we usually do is: **double dial plus text, two times a day, for the first three days. Then it goes down to once a day until day seven. And then it goes into the long-term [retargeting/email] list.**"

The attendee benchmarked their own numbers against this and the speaker confirmed they were on par: **~80% of bookings happen within 3 days; ~92% within 14 days.**

From the freight hot seat, the speed-to-lead and show-rate protocol:

- **Someone calls the lead within sixty seconds.**
- **Get them booked within three days** into the calendar (late bookings die).
- **Send an email the day before the call:** "Hey, we're actually blocking someone for your call tomorrow" — make them feel a little more committed and comfortable.

### Funnel architecture (ACQ's own funnel as the model)

> "Our cold traffic we target with the [lead magnet/event]. **The workshop is actually middle-funnel for us.** Then we **retarget all of the people that filled out the lead magnet — they qualified into the lead magnet — with the webinar.**"

So: cold traffic → qualified lead magnet opt-in (pixel on qualified event) → retargeting sequences push them to the mid-funnel conversion event (workshop/webinar) → sales team. Each next step in the funnel gets **pre-framed with a VSL**: "Every time you have a next step in the funnel, you should pre-frame it with the VSL — pre-frame the conversation, kill some of the zombies or objections they might have, attract the right audience and push away the audience that is not [right]." (Alex has published frameworks for building the VSL — "you can go look online, there's a bunch of frameworks Alex explained on how to build the perfect VSL.")

## Part 1 Hot Seats

### Hot seat 1: ABO→CBO mechanics and pixel/lookalike stacking

**Context:** Attendee loved test-with-ABO and asked two questions: (1) when a winner emerges from an ABO ad set of 3–5 variants, does the winner get its own ad set in the CBO? (2) Is there a big difference between optimizing on the qualified-thank-you-page conversion event vs. supplementing with a lookalike audience seeded from CRM-tagged leads?

**Prescription:**
- The winner goes into a **full CBO campaign for its angle** — the campaign where you're "throwing all of the [winning] creatives for that [angle]."
- Don't choose between the qualified-event pixel and lookalikes — **combine them**: broad audience (creative is the targeting) + qualified conversion event + lookalikes + **CAPI as the third layer** feeding actual purchases from the CRM back to Meta.

### Hot seat 2: Financial-services webinar funnel (opt-in rate / CPL problem)

**Context:** Runs challenge/webinar offers for financial services — taxes, M&A, stock-trading courses. Funnel: ad → free webinar opt-in form → results page selling a **$27 product** before the webinar → **live webinar 1–2×/week** → pitch for a **$200 ticket**. Show rate **20–35%**; conversion of attendees **12–20%** (highest 20, lowest ~12). Spending **$3–5K**. Webinar registrants get email reminders but **no calls**. Struggling with opt-in rate and cost per lead.

**Prescription:**
1. **First change: the landing page.** Put a **VSL at the top** — use the sales letter to pre-frame the webinar, kill objections, attract the right audience and repel the wrong one. Use Alex's published VSL frameworks.
2. On the "should each niche get its own funnel" question — **no**. Go super narrow at the **ad** level (ACQ uses per-industry static images with direct callouts), but keep **one landing page** because the avatar is one; the ad is what you personalize.
3. (Implicit from the funnel review: registrants aren't being called — the nurture/call cadence above applies.)

### Hot seat 3: Marriage coaching — $200K/month spend, 25% decline, degrading lead quality

**Context:** Scaled from zero to ~$1M/month by 2023, managed decline since, **~25% decline over the last three months**. Sells a marriage-coaching offer. 80% paid traffic; ~85% of that Meta, remainder Google. Funnel: ad → opt-in page with VSL → application form → routing to high-ticket or low-ticket sales team based on answers, with the conversion sent to Facebook. Problem: audience quality has shifted from entrepreneurs/business owners/corporate people to mostly blue-collar; leads claim $75K/year income on the application but "show up to the call and don't have five hundred dollars to their name." Couldn't tell if it was creative, a competitor skimming high-income leads, or pixel conditioning.

**Prescription:**
1. **"My guess would be it's on the creative side."** Analyze all of the creatives that brought in the high-income clients, find what those creatives have in common (concept and angle), and **double down on that concept/angle**.
2. They said they'd tried that — 100 creatives tested per month, only 5–6 durable winners. Answer: **"You need to test more."** This is where the volume rule was stated: **10 new creatives/week per $10K/month of spend** (at $200K/month they were testing at ~1/8 of the implied rate).
3. On "should high-income targeting get its own pixel and separate funnel": **pick the high-income audience and speak only to them.** "I think that your strategy should only speak to [the high-income avatar] today" — don't split focus trying to serve both ends of the market at once.

### Hot seat 4: Freight logistics — show-rate fix

**Context:** Logistics/freight brokerage offer: a **free "measurer" device** (pallet-scanning) as the front-end offer. ~**8 meetings/day, ~40/week**. Few months into running ads, not yet disqualifying leads. **55%+ book a meeting, but show rate is the problem.**

**Prescription:** "I would focus on that — that's the first thing." Understand why they don't show (booked too deep into the calendar, not called right away). The fix, verbatim sequence:
1. **Add a VSL** (pre-frame the meeting).
2. **Have someone call them within sixty seconds** of the lead coming in.
3. **Book the call within three days** on the calendar — no distant bookings.
4. **Email the day before:** "we're actually blocking someone for your call tomorrow" — commitment and comfort.
5. Accept that adding qualification will push total lead count down, then **increase spend** to recover volume at higher quality.

### Hot seat 5: Functional medicine — the $9 good-friction price

**Context:** Functional-medicine lab offer: **free lab test, just pay shipping & handling ($17.95; average cart ~$37 with bumps/post-checkout offers)**. Brings in **5,000–6,000 new customers/month** but: test **completion rate only 55–60%**, and of those only **~20% show up for their results call**. Prospects are skeptical ("why would you give out a free lab?"). Stuck at the ~5,000/month scale ceiling; losing **$35–40 per sale** on the front end; **doesn't break even until month six**. Tried "50% off" at $49 — didn't scale.

**Prescription:**
1. **"Can you make it very cheap so you create a little bit of friction? Make it nine dollars instead of free.** Because when you give something like that away for free, **people don't believe it and they don't use it.**"
2. Not free, and not $49 — **"something in the middle... nine dollars is cheap enough that people are going to buy it, and you put a little bit of good friction into the funnel."**
3. Framing options: **"80% off at nine dollars"**, or a **launch offer** — "worth $100, we're going to give it to you for $9 because we're launching [a new thing]."
4. **Use the VSL on the landing page to explain WHY it's $9 instead of $300** — the price story kills the skepticism that's suppressing completion and show rates.

**Lesson: free maximizes lead volume and minimizes lead commitment. A small paid commitment ("good friction") filters for people who will actually complete the process and show up.**

### Hot seat 6: Austin dental group — geo-targeted Meta for weak locations ⭐ THE MULTI-LOCATION LOCAL-SERVICES TEMPLATE

**Context:** Dentist with a **10-location dental group in Austin**. Was stuck at **$1M/month for two years**; optimized and scaled **PPC (Google)** over six months and got to **$1.5M/month** — but the gains all came from winners becoming "super winners": **5 offices are crushing it, the 5 that suck still suck, and 2 locations are essentially dead** (one has a genuinely bad, hard-to-find location). Does no Meta at all. Question: "Will Meta, targeting those geographic areas with the creative of the ads, fill the chairs at those locations?" — noting PPC's geographic targeting is broader/blunter.

**Prescription (this is the template — flag it):**
1. At ~$1M+/month you've earned the right to **open up additional lead-generation channels** — add Meta alongside PPC.
2. Run a **free-something (or low-priced good-friction) front-end offer** — similar to the lab-test model — **geo-targeted ONLY to the areas around the underperforming locations**. **"Into those [full] locations, you don't show the ad."**
3. That fills the chairs at the weak locations, and then you **upsell into the full diagnostic** (and ongoing treatment) on the back end.
4. **"Make sure to call out the ideal avatar"** in the creative so the geo-targeted ad still trains Andromeda on the right people, not just the right zip codes.

**Why this matters for any multi-location or geography-bound local-services business:** channel mix per location, not per company. Search (PPC/LSA) harvests existing demand and disproportionately feeds your already-strong locations; Meta with a front-end offer *creates* demand and can be aimed surgically at the specific geographies where you have capacity. Suppress delivery where you're full; concentrate spend where chairs are empty; use a compelling low-friction front-end offer plus avatar callouts; monetize on the back end.

## Part 2: Leadership Systems and A-Player Teams

### Framing: at this revenue band, the constraint is "who," not "how"

> "At your revenue band, the un-fucking in your business is less about you doing it, and more about figuring out **who** needs to do it — putting the right people in the right seats and making sure they are pointed in the right direction... All of the problems in your business are less tactical-and-how, and more about who do I need to put here, who do I need to recruit, who do I need to place so the business can move at the speed I want toward the destination I want."

Supporting story: before Gym Launch was sold to private equity, they ran a **retroactive analysis over all their Gym Launch data** and found that **every season of maximum growth never came from a campaign or a strategy — it always came from a who they hired to do the thing.**

### The car metaphor (diagnosing which system is broken)

Your business is a system of four systems, like a car:

| Car system | Business system |
|---|---|
| Fuel system | **Marketing** |
| Engine | **Sales** |
| Wheels & alignment | **Operations** |
| Navigation | **Leadership** (most important — it means you know you're going the right direction) |

"The way you know your business is working well is that it is moving at the speed and direction that you want." The owner's job is to be **the mechanic**: diagnose the correct broken system. "If you brought your car to a mechanic with a clearly flat tire and they said you needed a new engine, you'd take your car to a different mechanic. Same thing in your business — we have to clearly diagnose what the problem is."

### The A-player definition

Two characteristics define **A-player potential**:

1. **Critical thinking** — can they think outside the box, think strategically about complex problems?
2. **Coachability** — can you actually mold their behavior the way you want?

Plus, for people hired to run something now (advisors, leaders): **they have done the thing you need done, at scale.** "If we are bringing in a business development advisor, we go look at their history and pattern: how they actually built scaled companies, how they ran operations, how they ran sales teams, and how they solved the problems they're going to face in the current role."

Key economics: **"A-players don't start out as A-players, but they have the characteristics for it."** If you get an A-player earlier in their career, you get them **cheaper**, and you get to install your methods. Examples given: **Ed** (long tenure, heavy surface time with Alex, now leads the entire workshop department) and **Jacob Hopkins** (started with Alex at 16 pitching software; Alex told him "your software sucks and your sales suck... you should probably learn how to sell better"; he learned, showed grit, was recruited into Gym Launch, scaled through it, and now leads the entire sales department at 23).

They also want the **"FIO quality — figure it out"** — because whoever you hire will face brand-new problems three to six months in.

### Interview mechanics

- **Give unsolvable problems** in the interview process to see how candidates think.
- **Sales roles: the unclosable-prospect role-play.** Give them a script, then play a prospect armed with every possible objection. "If they get flustered, if they shut down — immediate red flag. If they try to solve it, talk through it, think through it — really good signal."
- **Immediately give sincere-candor feedback** (a core value) and watch what happens: "If they take that feedback, maybe implement it and try again — that's a really good signal." This is the coachability test.
- **Managers / business development / EVP and above: the business diagnosis.** Give them business details with **not enough information** to solve the problem and say "find the constraint." Watch how they think tactically. **Test this at multiple points in the interview process.**
- **High friction throughout:** lengthy interview process plus a **two-day onsite** — day one shadowing, day two a **live presentation on stage** in front of the hiring team, "where we are going to be very violent with the feedback that we give." Then they **wait until Monday** to decide.
- **The litmus test:** "Based on what we know about this candidate, **would we hire ten of them?** And if it is not a definitive 'fuck yes' from everybody on the team, they don't make it."

### Brand as your talent-acquisition funnel

> "At your revenue band, your number one constraint will be A-player attraction. **Your brand is your talent acquisition funnel** — the same way you have a client acquisition funnel that goes broad and gets narrower and puts healthy friction on the front end, you want the same thing for A-player attraction."

Tactics:

1. **A careers/application page that leads with vision, core values, and mission — preferably with a VSL from the founder.** Model: **acquisition.com/careers** (what Alex put up). Vision/mission/values come **before** compensation on the page, because "**A-players are driven toward vision, mission, and impact before compensation.**" (Example: Ned, VP of a product line, had a **nine-figure exit** — the day the check hit his account he was in the office interviewing, because "it took me four and a half years to build a nine-figure company; you guys did that in seventy-two hours with the book launch. What would it be like to play on that team?")
2. **The "poach, not poke" strategy.** Once the careers page + VSL exists: find talent that would be a great fit based on their social media or the product they've been working on, and send a **direct message** (sometimes from Alex's account, sometimes from Layla's): "*Love what you're doing / the impact you're having. **If you know anybody like you**, here's a page describing the position we're hiring for.*" "When we use that mechanic, we typically see **that person** apply for the role." The indirect ask preserves face and converts the target themselves.
3. **Always-on pipeline.** "There's always room for [talent]." They maintain a talent-acquisition pipeline at all times: a **community and jobs board** that explicitly says "*If we're not hiring now, we'll probably hire in the future — please submit your information.*" Fill the pipeline **before** you need the role.
4. **For sales roles specifically: "drop an atomic bomb"** — deploy every front-end strategy at once to flood the funnel so you can interview many and take only the best: the poach-not-poke DMs, **direct Facebook recruiting ads with a callout** ("*If you are a top performer, a top salesperson, and you're struggling being in an atmosphere where you're the best and everybody else in the room is relatively average — this might be a good fit for you*"), recruiters, LinkedIn, Indeed. The **direct callout from the founder's brand** is what they've seen work best.

### Sales org structure and compensation

- **Everyone starts as a setter — no exceptions for closers.** "Every salesperson, no matter how great they were in a previous role, starts out as a setter" — doing volume at the lowest-level job, sitting **twenty-minute qualifying calls**, before leveling up to Closer 1. **There are three closer tiers.** Why: break bad habits, indoctrinate into the process, teach the system, and test grit. Even **top closers making $50–80K/month elsewhere** come in as setters — "because the brand is so powerful." (The exception: a **sales manager** hire doesn't start as a setter — they've already proven concept elsewhere; that's a leadership-level hire, validated and paid as such.)
- Salespeople's two bad reputations that this filters out: **offer-hopping** (never sticking anywhere) and **divas** (the best salespeople are often the hardest to manage).
- **W-2 base + commission, never 1099 commission-only.** "If they are commission-only 1099, we can't control what they do... Base-plus is how we can control them — morning meetings, end-of-day reports, following up the leads. We can tell them what to do."
- **Closer comp: base range $55–75K; on-track earnings $250–300K.**
- **Setters: also base plus commission**, with comp tied to **leading measures** — volume/output (number of appointments, number of shows, activity they can track) — with the **lagging measure (revenue)** as a second tier of the bonus. "We want to control leading measures... we know that if there is enough volume on the front end, [outcomes follow]."
- **The career ladder is the recruiting pitch** for taking a step back: show the ladder from setter to Closer 1/2/3 and the potential comp plan. "The thing about A-players is that we want to see progress — progress and impact. If you tell me I'll be in the same role for the rest of my life, you're going to get a button-pusher in that job." For someone currently earning $250K elsewhere: "**state the facts and tell the truth**" — there has to be a value stack (brand, skills, long-term earnings, vision) that is disproportionately more valuable long-term than what they're leaving.
- **Hiring leaders (managers, directors, department heads):** recruit people who have **already done the thing and solved the problems at a high level**, and pay accordingly — **20–30% above industry standard as a baseline, plus variable comp based on (typically team) performance.**
- **The "can I afford an A-player" question:** part math — what is the **LTGP cap (net return)** on this employee if placed? — and part the discount play: find the person with the characteristics **earlier in their career**, buy them at a discount, document them into your process, and give them the ladder.
- ACQ scale reference: ~**50 total salespeople, roughly 25 setters / 25 closers**, working leads like lead-magnet downloads ("someone downloads Alex's worksheet off the YouTube page — they'll call them, twice").

### The sales manager and the daily rhythm

> "**If you have more than four salespeople, you need a dedicated sales manager.**"

The ACQ sales manager's only job, same format every day, Monday through Friday:

**Morning (the verbatim morning-meeting template — 40 minutes to an hour, whole team on Zoom):**
1. **Win highlight** — open by getting the team to spotlight something that worked recently.
2. **Topic of the day** — one part of the script per day across the five days: **intro, opening, objection handling, discovery, etc.** Include objections that have been coming up over the last week.
3. **Manager demonstrates** the topic live.
4. **Role-play with the team** in real time.

**Middle of the day:** review **all** the sales team's calls against the script and performance standards; hold **one-on-ones**.

**End of day:** the **end-of-day report** — number of leading measures / activity done, plus outcomes as lag measures.

That's the whole job: morning meetings, call review, one-on-ones, EOD reports — the same weekly template, each day highlighting a different script section.

**Recording and grading calls:** record everything. For field/home-service teams not on Zoom: "**put an audio recorder on** [them] so we can actually audit the process." An attendee described uploading recordings to AI for script-based grading and recommendations — acknowledged as the direction, with the caveat that AI grading is imperfect; the ACQ answer is that the **sales manager's human review against the script remains the core mechanism**, with AI as leverage.

### Setter/closer handoff rules

- The **setter qualifies** (20-minute qualifying call), confirms fit, and **books the appointment** ("You're a good fit — I've booked you for Friday at two").
- **Once the appointment has been set, ownership moves to the closer.** If the lead no-shows, **it is the closer's job to reach out** — the lead is in the closer's nurture pipeline. ("Sometimes it'll reset back to the setter bucket, but it's in the closer's domain at that point.")
- Closers work their nurture pipelines **between calls when their calendar isn't fully booked**. In peak seasons closers take **two appointments per hour**; when closers are fully booked, **setters fill in** on the nurture follow-up.
- Governing principle: "**You want your closers closing.**"

### Org design: the three R's + the time study

Asked "which role should be my number one hire" by a founder juggling CEO/CMO/partnerships:

> "**Fire everybody in your business, including you.** Build the structure for future scale — what does it need to look like eighteen months from now, or at three-to-four-times the revenue? What would that system need to look like operationally? **Then** think about putting people in the seats."

For every seat, define the three R's:
1. **Role**
2. **Responsibilities** (what they manage)
3. **Results** (what they're held accountable to)

Then audit yourself against the structure ("I'm best at this — that's the seat I need to sit in"), and **backfill everything that isn't high-leverage for you**.

**The time study** (called "the highest-ROI thing you can take away from this workshop"): clearly document, top-down and bottom-up, a **full leadership business bible of what everybody in the business is doing** — then use AI as a thinking partner against it: Who should I hire for? What is efficient/inefficient? What should become an automation or a workflow? What KPIs am I actually holding each department to? That becomes a strategic playbook for hiring. (Recording tools — call recording, wearable recorders — make capturing the raw material passive: "if it's recording your sales calls all day, all of that becomes the details.")

### ACQ leadership operating rhythm

- **Everyone on the leadership team personally owns a metric.**
- **"There is no meeting without an agenda, sent in advance."** Every meeting is designed to **solve a problem and come out with a solution that gets tested in the field.**
- **Few meetings** overall — "all of our leaders know how to get shit done tactically; the last thing you want to do is waste time on the meeting calendar."
- **Quarterly reset:** every quarter, Alex, Leila, and Sharad recast the vision for the next ninety days and set priorities, which cascade down through departmental leaders and managers to the whole team.
- Philosophy: "**We are less managers and more outcome creators.**" The culture works because of the **friction in the hiring process** — it is not easy to get a job there, so management overhead is low.
