# Workshop Lesson 07 — Sales Philosophy, Scripting, and Lead Management

**Source:** Acquisition.com / Mozi workshop roundtable, 2026-08-20. Hosted by Pat, Acquisition.com's sales leader.

This session was a sales-motion roundtable, not a sales-skills training — and that distinction is the whole point. Pat opened with the story of his first meeting with Alex Hormozi, who told him the difference between their philosophies: Pat's instinct was to make *people better at sales* through skills, training, and coaching; Alex's first instinct is to *make sales easier for people* by engineering the motion upstream. From that frame, Pat covered why Acquisition.com runs every lead — warm or cold — through one identical cold-built funnel, how thousands of sales-call transcripts are mined into a "Why People Buy" document that drives their hooks, creatives, and content, how they train a 70/30 listen/talk ratio through a "great first date" clarifying-question drill, how speed-to-lead (calling within 60 seconds) works, and how trust and proof — not longer pitches — shorten sales cycles for high-risk purchases. A workshop Q&A illustrates how a complex sales motion can be made easier through pre-purchase education and a focused discovery process; adapt that principle only where research and relevant professional rules permit.

---

## Lessons & Frameworks

### 1. Make sales easier — don't (just) make people better at sales

The meta-frame for the whole session. Pat: *"The way you think about sales is you try to make people better at sales through skills and training and coaching. And there's a place for that. But the way that I think about it, first and foremost, is how do I make sales easier for people?"* (Alex Hormozi, quoted by Pat.)

The implication: before investing in sales training, coaching, or hiring "better closers," fix the *motion* — the funnel structure, the pre-call assets, the qualification, the script architecture, the lead routing. A mediocre salesperson inside a well-engineered motion beats a great salesperson improvising inside a broken one. Every downstream question in the session (VSLs, transcript mining, talk ratios, speed-to-lead, productization) is an application of this one idea: remove work from the sales conversation by doing it earlier, systematically, and in a copyable form.

Pat illustrated the tension with a marketing-workshop attendee who complained: *"Dude, I just have my credit card. I was ready to go. You made me get on the phone with a salesperson, go through the whole sales process. Why the hell did you do all that?"* On the surface, forcing a ready buyer through a 30–40 minute sales call looks like the opposite of making sales easier. The answer is the next lesson.

### 2. "Hope for warm, prepare for cold" — one funnel, one script, for everyone

Most businesses build their sales motion when their leads are referrals and warm inbound. Then they grow, lead quality shifts colder, and they conclude: *"I have a lead problem. I have a qualified lead problem. I can't get more leads. I've got all the wrong people."* Pat's diagnosis: *"No, you have a sales motion problem. You don't have a lead problem."* Their sales motion is built for warm; the frame is **hope for warm and prepare for cold** — build the motion for the coldest lead you'll ever get, and run everyone through it.

Why run even warm, credit-card-in-hand buyers through the full cold funnel?

1. **You capture the buying psychology every time.** The cold funnel forces discovery of: why they're here in the first place; their current state; the problem they're trying to solve; what solving it actually looks like; their future desired state; the obstacles to getting there; and their past experiences (*"We hired an agency and it fucking sucked"* / *"We tried to run ads, and we dumped fifty K, and we didn't know what we were doing"* / *"We tried to figure it out internally. We didn't have the skills"*). You want the pain point — **internal pain (how they feel) and external pain (what they're going through)** — regardless of warm or cold.
2. **Objections differ by temperature.** Warm-audience objections are very different from cold-audience objections; one process surfaces and handles both.
3. **All of it feeds the "Why People Buy" document** (Lesson 3) — skipping the funnel for warm buyers would starve the marketing intelligence loop.
4. **One process = operational leverage.** Pat: *"It's the same process, regardless of whether or not they were warm or cold. No one's treated differently; no one follows a different script. It's the same script for everybody."* Consequences: onboarding new salespeople is easier; diagnosing a broken step is easier; KPIs are easier to track; and replacing someone who leaves or can't hack it is easy *"because we're not looking for somebody that's doing something differently than everyone else."*

### 3. The "Why People Buy" document — transcript mining as the marketing engine

Acquisition.com takes **all sales-call transcripts — thousands and thousands of them** — and runs them through an internal document called **"Why People Buy."** What gets extracted:

- **Keywords** — the literal words prospects use
- **Problems and pain points** — tallied by frequency, split into **internal pain** (feelings: how they feel about the situation) vs. **external pain** (how it's affecting their life)
- **Future desired states** — where they want to be
- **Obstacles** they face getting there
- **Past experiences** — what they tried before and why it failed
- **Most common objections**
- **Avatar and qualifier data** — at Acquisition.com: revenue, profit, business type; used at discovery to answer "how many calls are we getting from home-service companies? how many with marketing issues vs. lead issues?"

**Where it routes:** the document goes to the head of marketing and the head of content (Julian), and it informs **hooks, creatives, and content**. Because they market to a cold, problem-aware audience, they use **pain-based hooks**: *"we look for what are the most common words we hear in terms of pain points... we kind of tally that in terms of those common words. And then we use those words"* — e.g., "struggling to scale" for a scaling workshop. It even feeds Alex's live-stream content: the questions Alex answers on stream often come straight from sales calls, and the businesses chosen for live diagnostics are picked to match the avatar the sales team is selling to. They also record the workshop roundtables themselves and feed those transcripts to marketing to target based on *"what we're hearing live in the room."*

**The prompt pattern** Pat gave for anyone to replicate this: *"If you just say, hey [LLM], what are the most common things needed in order to build a great marketing campaign in terms of these details, and then say, 'Here is the transcripts. What are you seeing based upon those common things?' You'll get your answers."* I.e., two steps: (1) ask the LLM what inputs a great campaign needs (pains, desired outcomes, objections, language), then (2) hand it the transcripts and ask it to extract exactly those things.

**Why this closes the loop on Lesson 1:** *"It makes sales easier because if we're targeting the right avatar upstream, by writing the right hooks, calling out the right pain points... my team is fully prepared... and they're getting a much higher quality of time."* The colder the audience gets and the less it's a hype sell, the more this intelligence matters.

### 4. 70/30 talk ratio and the "Great First Date" clarifying-question drill

A wealth-of-detail answer to a room question ("my call's an hour and I'm talking about seventy percent of it — should we change the script?"):

- **The rule:** *"You maintain around a seventy thirty talk track... thirty percent of the time you are talking, seventy percent of the time you are just hearing what they have to say."* (He also mentioned 40/60 as a bound.) Rationale: *"People want to talk. People want to say what they want to say. They're not that interested in what you have to say. And a lot of times we over pitch features... really people just want help. Can you solve my problem?"* Governing rule of thumb: **"you cherish information"** — the more information you gather by talking less, the better.
- **The training drill — "Great First Date":** Pat role-played a Tinder first date with a volunteer, twice.
  - **Wrong way (robotic script-marching):** "What do you like to do for fun?" → "The beach." → "What do you like to eat?" → "Italian." → "Where did you go to college?" → "Harvard." → "Oh, I went to Penn State..." Back-and-forth, robotic, no learning, and the salesperson fills silence talking about themselves.
  - **Right way:** *"Within every question, we ask as many clarifying questions as possible until we need to get to the next question."* Same opener ("What do you like to do for fun?") became: How long have you lived in San Diego? → Favorite beach? → What do you like about it? ("Fewer people, fewer crowds") → Do you surf? → Do you bring snacks? → Anything active? — and only *then* naturally bridged to the next scripted question ("ever feeling hungry after? What do you like to eat?").
  - The script is a skeleton of anchor questions; the skill is drilling clarifying questions inside each anchor before moving on. That's how you invert the talk ratio without abandoning the script.

### 5. Speed-to-lead: 60 seconds, staffed for it

Asked to "rant on speed-to-lead":

- **The 60-second rule:** *"If you are calling within sixty seconds of getting your lead to opt in for something, the likelihood that you are actually going to get that lead to be interested... and go through your process and take you up on your offer is a lot higher. The faster you get to a lead, the better."*
- **Speed-to-lead and show rate are "two of the same"** — the same discipline drives both.
- **The early touch is discovery + qualification**, not a pitch: *"What made you download it? What's the interest to you? What problems do you face? How can we help?"* It's also a trust touch: *"Can I get a touch point ahead of time with somebody to start to build some trust and rapport... and increase the likelihood that person is going to be interested in what I have to buy downstream."*
- **Staffing implication:** Acquisition.com covers multiple time zones (they have people in Ireland) — *"we want to make sure that we're available for multiple different time zones and times of day, where somebody can get in touch with somebody else."* If the phone can't be answered in 60 seconds during the hours leads arrive, the rule is fiction.
- For the questioner (audiobook downloads where 40% volunteer a phone number but nobody calls them): stop debating funnel variants — *"I would just focus on getting on the phone and actually figuring it out."*

### 6. High-risk purchases: trust + proof, not longer sales calls ("time is trust")

A wealth manager asked how the sales process should change when the "purchase" is 80–90% of a person's life savings — should you budget 5, 10, 20 hours per lead? Pat reframed it as two questions: **"How do I improve the factor of trust in the business?"** (people decide based on how much they trust you) and **"How do I increase the perceived likelihood of success?"** (i.e., reduce perceived risk).

- **Trust = brand + time.** The questioner's own observation, which Pat endorsed: prospects who buy a $5K ticket have often consumed dozens or hundreds of hours of content first — *"What is time? Time is trust."* Brand does the same job: *"It's much easier for somebody to go spend three hundred dollars on a Balenciaga shirt simply because it's a Balenciaga shirt... All that is is trust."* Build brand equity so the buying decision crosses a veil of trust that was earned *before* the call.
- **Perceived likelihood of success = proof.** *"Just violent amounts of proof that you guys are producing results with people that look very similar to them in terms of their demographic."* Even in regulated industries with limits on claims, "that doesn't mean that proof can't be shown at all."
- **The 1h15m heuristic:** From Acquisition.com's "boardrooms": *"If you are going over an hour and fifteen minutes in delivering that strategy, you don't understand the strategy well enough."* Applied to sales: if your call runs two hours, *"we haven't put enough trust in place ahead of time, and we haven't reduced the risk enough ahead of time, to the point where we feel as though we have to overcompensate... it's more words, more compensation."* A long call is a symptom of a trust deficit upstream, not diligence. The scripting goal is always: *"how do we reduce the amount of the script? How do we reduce the size?"*
- Result: solving trust + proof upstream *"will allow you to shorten the overall sales cycle tremendously because that's what people care about: that they trust you and that they're not putting their money at a high level of risk."*

### 7. Pitch outcomes, not process — menu upsell and productization

From the freight-software exchange (Lesson expanded in Q&A below):

- **Discovery stays at the beginning; there is a defined pitch point.** The best pitch is *"an offer that is a well defined offer that does not change by the person"* — you're just aligning their stated pain points with your value.
- **Outcomes, not process:** *"When you're pitching, you're speaking to outcomes, not process... Not the how I do it, but the what I do."* In a good VSL: *"We're not saying step by step, 'let me take you for a demo of all the things that we do for you.' We say, 'Here is our special sauce. Here is the different way we think... and here are the outcomes that we're going to get you.'"* Focusing on the what lets the pitch be much shorter.
- **The "menu upsell" frame-keeper:** Asking "do you want this? could you use that?" loses frame. Instead: *"Heard everything you talked about. Here's the list of all the things that we do. I crossed out the things you don't need. This is what you do need. Sound good?"* You maintain frame by prescribing based on professional experience — and *"if we show in that part of the pitch that we're confident enough to say these are the things you need, it makes the conviction... much higher. We'll increase the likelihood that someone will choose to buy based upon confidence."*
- **Productize the configuration:** Don't ask configuration questions on the close call. Know your avatar's most common needs, the features easiest to fulfill and highest in value, ship a standard package, and add extras later: *"This is what we've worked with so many businesses just like you. This is the best thing that works for them. Let's ship you this. If there is anything else that you need, we'll add it."* Going the other direction (à-la-carte questioning) loses frame and slows the sale.

## Q&A / Room Exchanges

### Exchange 1 — Scripting talk ratio (SaaS/agency attendee)

*"If I have a script and my call's an hour, about seventy percent of that time I am talking. Should we change the script?"* → Answer became Lesson 4: flip to 70% prospect / 30% salesperson; train it with the "Great First Date" clarifying-question drill; scripts are anchor questions, and inside each anchor you exhaust clarifying questions before advancing.

### Exchange 2 — Speed-to-lead (audiobook/webinar funnel attendee)

Their situation: prospects download a free audiobook, ~40% volunteer a phone number, but the firm never calls — they push to webinar then a free call. Pat's answer became Lesson 5: call within 60 seconds; use the first touch for discovery and qualification ("what made you download it? what problems do you face?"), which doubles as an early trust/rapport touch; staff for coverage across time zones; and stop theorizing — get on the phone and find out.

### Exchange 3 — High-risk purchase / wealth management

*"We ask clients to move eighty percent of their entire life savings... how do you analyze the time you should invest per lead?"* Pat refused the premise that bigger transactions need proportionally longer sales processes. Answer became Lesson 6: trust (brand + time-in-content: "time is trust") and perceived likelihood of success ("violent amounts of proof" from demographically similar clients) are what shorten the cycle; a 2-hour call means you failed to build trust upstream and are compensating with words; the 1h15m boardroom heuristic applies.

### Exchange 4 — THE DIVORCE FIRM EXCHANGE (director of marketing & systems, divorce law firm) ⭐

**The setup, verbatim from the questioner:** *"I am the director of marketing and systems for a divorce law firm... the managing partner is still taking all of the consults. He does all the consults, him and another attorney. And he wants to get out of that, but... he doesn't want to hire lawyers to do it. He wants to try the route of non-attorney salespeople, but people still ask legal questions in his consults. Non-attorney salespeople can't legally give legal advice."* She's already capturing his call scripts/recordings; she hears him through the office wall saying, every single call: *"Let me ask you some questions that'll help me help you"* — then asking the few specific questions that actually matter after letting the prospect ask theirs. The goal state: at the end of the call the prospect receives and signs a **fee agreement** and pays the **initial retainer**.

**Pat's restatement of the problem:** *"How do we take the technical aspects that a non-trained person or a non-accredited person can't answer out of a sales process?"* Today, the managing partner is answering upfront legal questions, and it's those answers that get the prospect comfortable saying "you're the right fit for us."

**The core prescription: "codify the technical part of the prospect process into an artifact."**

**Step 1 — Harvest the questions.** *"If I were to take all the calls that I have from that managing director, and I were to take all the transcripts of all of my top prospects, top avatars, and pull out what are the fifteen most common questions, ten most common questions people ask..."* He noted this is also a **targeting** question — who and how wide are you targeting — but for divorce specifically: *"If it's divorce, there are fifteen commonly asked questions... or fifteen areas of clarification that they need."* So: mine the managing partner's consult recordings for the **10–15 most-asked questions**.

**Step 2 — Move those answers in front of the call.** Two options:

- **Option 1 (form + AI document):** Prospect fills out a form ("what questions do you have?"). The form passes into an LLM/AI, and *"the answers to those questions are based upon the way that the call recordings answer the questions previously"* — i.e., the AI answers in the attorney's own recorded style, from his actual past answers. Delivered back as a document: *"this is the document that I'm going to give to you to answer your question."*
- **Option 2 (pre-call VSL — Pat's preferred):** *"I actually kind of like this better... break it down to the five to ten most common questions you get, put those in a video sales letter or something along those lines that is addressed before somebody gets on a call, and have the person that is actually accredited and able to do it giving the answers to those questions"* — the accredited attorney answers them once, on video, ahead of time instead of live on every call. Length per Pat: **"five, seven, ten minutes, whatever it's gonna be. You build it out, you script it out. And then you send it to them before they get on the call."**

**Step 3 — The non-attorney call becomes pure discovery.** *"And then the rest is just discovery pain points."* Pat's analogy: it's like a prospect who watched the VSL before a sales call — *"'I had a bunch of objections, but actually the video sales letter answered all of them, so I am good to go.'"* The artifact must be something *"that can be copied and pasted easily"* — the point of codification is that the managing partner's expertise becomes infinitely replicable without his calendar. The non-attorney salesperson runs discovery and pain points to the fee agreement and initial retainer.

**Step 4 — VSL enforcement on the call (verbatim script).** The questioner asked: what if they didn't watch the VSL — do we say "go watch it, I'll wait"? Pat: *"That's what we do here."* The exact script:

> *"Did you watch? — Oh, no, I didn't. — Well, okay, in order for you to get the most value out of this phone call so that we can serve you in the best way possible, and to give me the most context so you can make the best decision for you and your future, what I think is best is if you watch this video. I am going to grab a coffee — or I can stay here on the phone with you, and we can go through it. That sound good? Cool, great, awesome."*

Then: **"Play it, leave it."** The VSL is not optional pre-reading; it's a gate enforced at the top of the call, framed entirely around the prospect's benefit.

**Step 5 — Deflection process for unanswerable legal questions.** Pat acknowledged: *"There will be friction. There will be a time in which there are certain questions where you say, 'I simply can't answer that.' And the process that we need to put in place for that is: how do we deflect that while still showing the value and putting them in a position where they feel comfortable to make the decision regardless?"* The questioner supplied the pattern she'd already seen working, which Pat endorsed ("Right"): **"Well, of course we handle that case. We've handled X amount of cases like that."** — i.e., answer the *competence* question (can this firm handle my situation?) with volume-of-proof, without answering the *legal* question (what will happen in my situation?), which is reserved for the attorney meeting.

### Exchange 5 — Freight/logistics software (discovery placement, pitching, productization)

Their script: first 20 minutes pure questions/prospect talking, then "here's how we solve that," with the back half all salesperson talking through features and live customization ("Do you want this? Do you want that? Do you want to provide a sales order number?") toward a free-trial close with card on file. Pat's answers became Lesson 7: keep discovery at the beginning; pitch a fixed offer aligned to their stated pains; speak to outcomes not process/features; replace à-la-carte feature questioning with the **menu upsell** ("here's everything we do, I crossed out what you don't need, this is what you do need"); and **productize** the default configuration based on the avatar's most common needs, shipping the standard package and adding features later. The diagnosis for their customization questioning: *"You are losing frame by starting to ask them what they need versus telling them."*
