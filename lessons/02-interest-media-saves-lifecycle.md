# Workshop Lesson 02 — Interest Media, Niche Content, the Saves KPI, and Life Cycle Ads

**Source:** Acquisition.com (ACQ) / Mozi marketing workshop, session recorded 08-19. Speaker: **Pat Bauer** — introduced as a former VP at a boutique investment bank ($200M in equity investments across nine countries, a $1B+ acquisition at G Capital) and ACQ's primary exit/go-to-market advisor. Format: solo lecture with heavy slide walkthroughs, live audience polls and call-backs to returning attendees (this was ACQ's third marketing workshop), one played video ad (the Gym Launch "one year later" testimonial), and a teaser for the next session ("how to use organic content to scale paid ads" and the Hook–Retain–Reward editing system). Core thesis: social media is dead, "interest media" is alive — the algorithm, not your follower count, decides who sees your content, so the content itself IS the targeting, and the only thing that matters is quality and volume of avatar-specific content.

---

## Lessons & Frameworks

### 1. Old world vs. new world (social media → interest media)

- **Old world (social media):** "You post, and then you gain followers from your posts. From there, you can make an offer to your followers, and that was how you made money." Big follower count = big reach for offers.
- **New world (interest media):** "You post; the algorithm does the work for you." The algorithm finds "anyone that would be interested in the things that you are posting about" — people already watching similar content — and feeds it to them. "What this allows us to do is make offers to both followers and non-followers," and "the amount of offers that we can make to the non-follower base is a lot bigger than we think. That's how we make money."
- Bottom line, verbatim: **"Followers don't matter anymore."**
- **Proof — Layla Hormozi's Instagram:** her most-viewed reel did **7.6 million views**. She has roughly **1.5 million followers**. Audience guesses of the follower share of those views: 1%, 4%. Actual answer: **about 6%**. ~94% of viewers had never clicked follow — the algorithm fed the content to non-followers.
- Consequence: **"The content is the targeting now."** You make content for your avatar; the creative itself is the audience targeting. "In order to win in today's world, it requires a lot more volume of the right content. Or as we prefer to say, quality and volume."

### 2. Volume benchmarks

- The cadence ladder Pat walked through: valuable content for your avatar **every day** → **multiple times per day** → **on multiple platforms** → multiple times per day on multiple platforms.
- ACQ's internal benchmark: **~1,000 pieces of content per week — each — for Alex, Leila, and Sean** ("Just kidding actually each. We make a thousand pieces per week each for them"). That is the scale of clipping/repurposing they run per personal brand, across multiple platforms.
- Do NOT chase this by imitating entertainers: "don't try to be like Mr. Beast or Kabi Lane or Charlie D'Amelio or jump on viral trends that don't connect to your business or the avatar at all. It might get you interest in terms of views, but doesn't connect to the avatar in terms of providing them real value."

### 3. Virality vs. business results

- **Marisol** — sells on Etsy, **2,400 followers**, one TikTok post did **2.9 million views**.
- **Marcus** — teaches building AI agents, **3,100 followers**, one TikTok post did **2.1 million views**.
- The question that matters: "did it help the business? **Virality doesn't necessarily equal business results.**" Going viral "does feel cool and you can leverage it," but the test is whether the content is "actually targeted towards the avatar that you're trying to service, and is it valuable for that avatar?"

### 4. Entertainer vs. educator money models

- **Two types of content:** entertainment and education. "Entertainment is meant to be consumed; it's meant to make us laugh, whereas **education is meant to change behavior**."
- **Entertainer model:** the entertainer performs for "a mass market of millions of strangers," but "the people that are actually paying them is the brands and the sponsors… They sell the attention to the brand and sponsor. So, **the audience is the product** and another company is paying to reach them." (Examples: Mr. Beast running down the road with a bag of money, Kathy Lam skits, pranks.)
- **Educator model:** "You take your knowledge, your experiences, and your unique perspective as it pertains to your business. You share that knowledge, you build trust, and position yourself as an expert in that subject matter category that aligns with your avatar." The niche becomes interested, buys, "and you make impact and income." Here **"the audience is the customer… we teach and sell to the same person."**
- Scale required: "Entertainers, they need a lot of strangers to make their money model work. Millions, whereas **you only need a few thousand to dominate your market**."
- Live illustration: "Who here Googled this to get here? Anyone Google 'marketing workshops in Vegas'?" — nobody. Everyone in the room came through the educational-content flywheel (got value from Alex/Layla's free content, applied it, saw results, then bought deeper).

### 5. The SAVES KPI — the #1 leading indicator before sales

- How do you know content is working? Sales, obviously — but the best **leading** indicator is not likes, comments, or shares ("Those are good, but they're not the number one thing").
- **"We found across a thousand plus educator-owned accounts that the single most important metric that generates revenue is actually saves."**
- Why: "You save something because it is valuable and you want to access it again later. Nothing has more of a direct ROI on whether or not your business is making money as a result of the content that you're creating than saves."
- Corollary case (what does NOT matter): **Megan**, a content creator posting "day in the life of a CEO," Amazon favorites, recipe favorites, self-care nights — her actual business is a **children's STEM tutoring academy**. Pat's prescription: "if her objective is to grow the business, she'd be posting **ninety-five percent or more** of her content being tutoring related."

### 6. The 2024 ACQ content failure (3× views up = 3× revenue down)

- In 2024, ACQ hired "a huge content team for Alex and Layla" that "had scaled many big-name entertainers" and promised to "blow this out of the water."
- Strategy: **broad top-of-funnel content** — the Alex-and-Layla relationship, powerlifting, weight gain/weight loss, and the "Ultra Mozi cookbook" (protein bombs, egg-white-and-protein-powder recipes).
- Result on vanity metrics: **"He tripled his overall views"** — more views, more impressions, more followers. "From a vanity success perspective, this was looking really good."
- Result on the business: **"Revenue three times down."** "These protein bombs… they're not going to make you come to a workshop." Alex's summary: "we tripled the vanity metrics and somehow, we made less money."
- The 2025 correction — "back to business": content went back to "simple makes money," how to win in business, the 4-hour sales masterclass, business diagnostics. **Views went down 3×, but revenue went up 3× from baseline** (not merely recovering from 2024 — 3× above the pre-experiment baseline).
- The lesson, verbatim: **"The right views, they make money, whereas the rest, they just make noise. They get in the way of actually serving your client."**

### 7. Oceans, lakes, and ponds — niche down and dominate

- Framework: "You've got oceans, lakes, and ponds. Now we want to be **the biggest fish in the smallest pond**. Our goal is not to serve everyone; our goal is to serve only a small group of people that are truly interested in what we buy."
- **Negative example — Synergy Martial Arts Academy:** self-defense, fighting, fitness, yoga, tai chi, discipline, kids classes, adults classes, women's-only classes, kettlebells. "No wonder it's empty. It's because they're trying to sell something to everybody… What do they even sell?"
- **The flip:** rebrand as **"Sarasota's number one kids martial arts academy" — "now the place is full."** "You're speaking only to one audience. It's clear exactly what you do."
- On the fear of niching too far — Layla's line: **"The missed opportunity is not a problem to solve."** The other opportunities will still be there (and probably bigger) later, and "with more money in your business… you are probably much better equipped to actually serve them in the future."
- **Gym Launch precedent:** sold to "a niche of a niche" — kickboxing, CrossFit, HIIT gyms. "**Total addressable market was only a hundred thousand. But we dominated that pond.**"

### 8. The podcast play (client acquisition via content)

- ACQ's tactic for getting new clients: "You try to bring people onto your podcast that are the types of people that you want to work with." Interview them about their business and "a trend or something that's happening in the market that's relevant to a lot of other people."
- At the end: **"You simply ask them, 'What ways can I serve you? Are there any ways that I can help you?'"** — you've already "edified yourself by asking the right questions, having an educational conversation with them where you show that you're an expert."
- Key reframe: **"It doesn't matter how many people watch it at all. What matters is that the right people are on it"** — and that the recording can be "sliced up in multiple ways and then turned into valuable content for a larger audience and let the actual algorithms do the targeting for you."

### 9. Small-account proof set ("you don't need a huge audience")

Pat's belief-break for founders with a couple thousand followers — three accounts:

| Account | Followers | Typical views | Business result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Temple Gains Fitness** (gym equipment; an account Alex personally follows and buys from — ACQ's ground-floor gym has their equipment) | **5,000** | 9K / 5K / 2K / 4K per post | **$6.5M in sales in under 36 months**, all through organic content |
| **Amy, "the reimbursement dietitian"** — helps registered dietitians make **$100/hour or more** by accepting insurance | **8,000** | Mostly small (a few hundred to tens of thousands; a couple of outliers) | **$1M/year at 80% margins** — "That's a good business… go buy yourself a vacation home" |
| **Ava** (ACQ client, ~19–20 years old) | **1,000,000** — but views of only 50K–70K per post | 1,500+ feed posts (not counting reels or other platforms); **zero followers, zero posts two years ago** | **Over $900K/month in revenue "with just a few people"** |
| | | | |

- The pattern in all three: "high quality content at volume, specifically for a very, very niche audience." Views look "low," but "the people that are watching are the **right** audience."
- "So dominate the pond, for a niche audience, and create for them just relentlessly."

### 10. "Capture, don't manufacture" — the 4 steps

For the founder saying "I just don't have the time to create all this content" (Pat polled the room — most people block out stressful "content creation time" each week):

> "We want to **capture, not manufacture** our content."

Four steps: **Look → Set up → Capture → Edit.**

1. **Look** — "Look at what's already happening in your business right now… moments before, during, and after." Physical products: unboxing. Services: sales calls, work in progress, delivery, satisfaction moments.
2. **Set up** — "Set up the environment to maximize the clip and minimize the editing." Alex's phrase, verbatim: **"An hour of pre is worth ten hours of post"** (pre-production vs. post-production). Engineer the capture in advance.
3. **Capture** — "You have your phone out before, during, after. Don't wait for the perfect moment." Alex now shoots much of his content on an iPhone, and "a lot of people prefer it because he's showing that you don't need high production value in order to create value."
4. **Edit** — with **HRR: Hook, Retain, Reward** (covered in the next session).

**Worked examples of capture:**
- **Teeth whitening clinic:** camera and "before" sign pre-staged; capture the client's face and uncertainty walking in (coffee stains, wedding coming up), then capture the after — "the emotional win, the excitement… the feeling of empowerment." Post the before/after; the algorithm feeds it to other people looking at teeth whitening; "the cycle goes around."
- **Online store (hair loss treatment):** spot the customer who has "purchased the same product six months in a row. Chances are that person is going to be a very good advocate." Camera pre-set, conversation scripted, right questions asked, then posted — "showing proof and reducing the risk" for the next buyer.
- **ACQ's own workshop system:** end-of-workshop survey → anyone who rates it 10/10 gets a text/call → **a scheduled testimonial call** with the camera already set up before Pat even walks in, Asana tasks pre-built, and **scripted questions used identically every time** ("Think about it like a sales script") so the clipping team "knows exactly where to clip and why." Bonus: the same calls surface workshop improvements — "it's a double dip."
- **Dion's man-on-the-street ads:** an ACQ director interviews attendees out front before and after the workshop — apprehension in, transformation out. "You might as well just put a camera in front of it and ask the right questions."
- **The flywheel:** "content targets prospects → the prospects become the customers → the customers then create more content and the flywheel goes around."

### 11. The five content formats

"The formats, they're just a reference; they're not a rulebook" — they work "across every industry: B2B, B2C, services, education, physical products, brick and mortar, software," and can be mixed and matched.

1. **Demonstration** — "let the product prove itself"; live use and reactions, unboxings, comparisons, before/afters ("they're doing the arguing for you"), high-production hero ads (the legendary **Dollar Shave Club** ad, which Gym Launch and many other brands copied), demonstrating live results (e.g., a packed gym).
2. **Testimonials** — UGC and direct-to-camera ("raw iPhone footage is going to beat that polished production. It feels real"), **podcast clips** ("School… runs thousands of ads, but ultimately some of the best-performing ads that we've had are clipped recordings from a podcast"), **parade of proof** (group testimonials — "volume of faces here is the argument"), walk-and-talk rants / man-on-the-street (Dion), influencer collabs, and **life cycle ads — "the deepest version of this format."**
3. **Education** — "teach first, provide value, and then sell": explainer videos that "teach one thing that matters to the buyer," **whiteboard breakdowns** ("super cheap to make, super effective, super long shelf life" — Alex does these constantly), how-to listicles, "proof, promise, plan — five steps to achieve X," and **repurposing top organic content into paid**: "**if it earned the views for free, it can earn clicks on spend.**" And crucially — actually ask: "if we truly believe that our content… is so valuable, what prevents us from asking people whether or not they want to take advantage of it? Asking for a CTA in our organic content. So many of us don't do this."
4. **Stories** — "a character, a problem, a turn"; lifestyle documentary, skits, brand manifestos. "**Stakes is the key here.** What's at stake for this business if they don't solve this problem?" And: **"Your taste, it's not the test"** — Alex originally disliked the lifestyle-documentary ad and "didn't believe that brand actually was important in growing a business… It performed anyway."
5. **Faceless** — screenshots of customer comments and texts run as ads on their own, text-only slideshows, animations, cartoons, video effects. "**There is no talent required for this. No founder on camera, no scheduling, no production day. It's a cheap variation machine**" — "you can slice and dice this fifty different ways… A/B test." "Look through your texts. What do you got in there? What emails do you have?… Can you remove their name and use that content… showing things happening real time?"

### 12. LIFE CYCLE ADS — one customer filmed across the entire journey (5 capture checkpoints)

- Definition: "**one customer filmed across the entire journey** — the entire customer journey… We're not only filming the moment after the win."
- Why it beats a standard testimonial: "A standard testimonial — it's just a snapshot of a point in time; you don't get the entire story. A life cycle ad acts like **the entire movie** for a client. **It's very hard to fake twelve months of footage.**"
- The mechanism: "The viewer, they **meet the skeptic, and then they watch them become the result**… Your competitors are a lot of times making the same big promise and guarantee. The proof really happens in the extended time that you show with your client." "When a prospect is shown that a company has stuck with a customer for two to three years… it provides all the proof you could possibly need."
- The narrative reference: **Rocky** — "Everyone loves a hero's journey… that comeback montage… raising his hands above his head in Philly. That's just cinematic genius, and it's also the reason that we like life cycle ads so much."

**The 5 capture checkpoints, in sequence:**

1. **The sales call** — "You record the call; we want to capture the doubts — **the doubts on tape — those become the opening scene** because the prospect sees themselves in this."
2. **Onboarding (day one)** — "Where do they start? What were their emotions? What were the questions… the apprehensions? They made the investment, now they're starting to feel like, did I make the right one? We capture that."
3. **The first win** — "the moment the product starts working for your client."
4. **The six-month update** — "What's the progress on camera? Where are they at? What are their big wins? What's changed for them?"
5. **One year later** — "the payoff scene, the transformation — they've reached that ideal future state."

- **The Gym Launch precedent:** "This is actually **the best campaign that Gym Launch ever ran** — the **one-year-later campaign**. Customers started as a skeptic on the sales call… you documented the transformation one year later." The hooks were the new customer's initial doubts: "The prospect hears **their own objections from the mouth of the new customer, and then watches the objections lose in real time**. I get chills just thinking about it."

### 13. The testimonial arc + the six scripted questions

Every converting testimonial "follows the same arc": **initial state → the doubt → what they did → the ideal future state.** The doubt is the linchpin: "the apprehension… 'Do I want to spend the money?' and then eventually saying, 'Screw it, I am going to do it.' What was going through their head at that time?"

**The six questions:**

1. **Who are they?** — Where do they live? What's their business?
2. **External before** — "What was the situation before? One to two short statements. What was their trouble? What was their pain? What was holding them back?"
3. **Internal before** — "How did they feel? Were they tired, frustrated, angry, scared? What couldn't they do with their life? **Who else in their life did it affect** — the kids, their family, their wives, their husbands, friends?"
4. **The bridge** ("this one's so important") — "How did they find you? What were their doubts? **What made them say yes regardless of those doubts?**"
5. **External after** — "Where are they at now? What can they do now that they couldn't do before? Who else is benefiting — your team, your staff, your family and friends?"
6. **The call to action** — "What would you tell someone who's unsure about the next steps?" Dion's field version: "For any founder that's watching this right now, that's unsure whether or not they want to come to this, what would you tell them?" — "it works so well because those founders that have already gotten the result feel the relation to that founder that felt that same way."

- Why the bridge matters, verbatim: "**The results alone, they will bounce off of a skeptic.** But when we highlight doubt of somebody that is a successful member of the program… **we label the exact fear, and then why they said yes anyway.** Super powerful."

### 14. The played Gym Launch one-year-later ad (the template in action)

Pat played the actual ad — a gym owner's testimonial that "made **tens of millions of dollars** on its own… it absolutely slapped." The beats, as spoken in the ad:

- **External/internal before:** "We were **two months from shutting our doors** when we started. We had about **thirty-one members**. We were making about **four grand a month**. Maggie was pregnant with our second. I had actually gone and gotten another job in order just to literally put food on the table… coaching in the morning, going to work, coaching in the evening… There was no light at the end of the tunnel."
- **The bridge / what they did:** "When we joined Gym Launch, we buckled down, and we followed the system, and **we followed it to a T**."
- **External after:** "**Eighteen months later**… I haven't coached a class in a year now. We've gone from **thirty-one members to over two hundred** easily… We literally went from being every day in our gym to **hiring a full staff and being able to step away in four and a half months**."
- **Internal after / who else benefits:** taking the kids to school, making dinner, gymnastics and soccer — "It's not easy, but it's completely worth it."
- **CTA (fully spelled out):** "I would encourage any gym owner that's sitting there on the verge… If that sounds interesting to you and you are willing to **risk one dollar**, all you have to do is **click the link, opt in, schedule a call time**."

### 15. CTA doctrine

- "**Don't make lazy CTAs.** Yes, that's the end of the statement."
- "Your audience only knows what to do if you tell them what to do." Spell it out step by step: "**click this button, call this number, reply yes, go to this site, scan this QR code**." Not just "hey, click."
- **Demonstrate the action:** "showing the click, then the form, then the submit — walk through the next steps on screen. **Congruence is shown in the click-through**, so they get exactly what they expected when they go through the process themselves."
- **Then layer in tactics:** "urgency, scarcity, guarantees, bonuses. That's what makes the CTA super-powered."
- "A great CTA strategy is the final step of a winning campaign. We can't forget that."

### 16. "New customers enter every day" + the Henry Ford poster

- Live proof: Pat polled who had attended an ACQ workshop before vs. who hadn't — the first-timers "are new customers, new opportunities that saw a lot of ads that we've run for a very, very long time."
- "**We get tired of our own stuff before the customers do**… New customers, they enter your market every day. **You owe them your highest-converting ad** so that you can help them, even if it's been running for a long time. Don't get bored repeating what works."
- "**You've seen it a hundred times. They've only seen it once.** Boredom is the founder's problem, not the market's." (Same logic applies to abandoning a working offer for a new business or new market.)
- **The Henry Ford story:** Ford had a new print ad of a beautiful car hanging in his office, walked past it daily, and one day announced "I am sick of this… time to scrap it and build something new." His assistant stopped him cold: "**Mister Ford, this hasn't even started running yet.** Just because you're sick of seeing the same ad doesn't mean you should change it."

### 17. Closing recap (verbatim)

"Social media is dead. Interest media is alive. Followers don't matter for business results. Going viral doesn't matter for business results. Trends don't matter for business results. **The only thing that matters is quality and volume for a niche.**" Next session: using organic content to scale paid ads (HRR editing).
