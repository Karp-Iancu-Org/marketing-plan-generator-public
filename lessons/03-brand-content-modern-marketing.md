**Source:** Acquisition.com/Mozi workshop transcript, "Lecture: Brand Strategy, Content Creation, and Modern Marketing." Primary speaker is Ed (Speaker 1), with an embedded Alex Hormozi segment (Speaker 2) — both a dissected clip of Alex's #1 YouTube video and Alex speaking directly about the YouTube highlights channel and post-Andromeda ad volume. ## Lessons & Frameworks

### 1. Brand = associations. Guard them or lose them. (Bud Light)

- **Core definition:** Your brand is the set of things people associate with you. Anything you attach your name to — a collaborator, an event, a spokesperson — transfers its associations onto you. The session opens with the warning: as your brand grows, more people reach out to "collab" because "they want to like rub their shit on you" — they're trying to siphon your audience and attach themselves to your associations. Acquisition.com screens workshop attendees for this reason; they turned away an agency for OnlyFans models — "Nothing morally against it, but like people would associate that type of business, not with the morals and ethics that we have in the company."
- **The Bud Light case (the numbers):** Bud Light's core associations — Clydesdales, blue-collar America, barbecues, Fourth of July. One influencer partnership that "just didn't match the brand" caused the core buyers to revolt:
  - Lost **$1.4 billion**.
  - Dropped from the **#1 beer spot they had held for two decades**.
  - Monthly sales: **$385 million/month before → $283 million/month after** — and "it has barely, barely recovered all the way up to now."
  - Repair strategy: partnering with the UFC and Shane Gillis — "That's on brand. That's America."
- **Lesson:** "You have to be very careful on what you associate your brand with... it's a multi-billion dollar mistake here." Associations are the asset. Protecting them is not optional hygiene; it's the whole game.

### 2. SPCL — the four pillars of influence ("influence maxing")

The formula for making a brand more influential. Four pillars — Status, Power, Credibility, Likeness (the speaker jokes it roughly spells "special"):

- **Status (S):** "You visibly control a resource that other people want." Money, attention, followers — earned or not, doesn't matter ("the trust fund kid that has lots of money even though he didn't earn it, he still has status"). Context-dependent: the bartender behind the bar has status (controls the alcohol); in the parking lot, nobody cares.
- **Power (P):** Called **"say-do correspondence"** — "you give instructions to your audience, they get the result that they want." Explicitly flagged as **the most important pillar for educators** (which is what a law firm doing content is): "Most important one for you guys here, because you guys are educators more than entertainers." The mechanism: "when it's like workshop ad from Hormozi, he already made me money from free stuff. He said do a thing; I made money. He says buy a thing; it's an easy decision." Free tactical instruction that produces real results makes the eventual paid offer a foregone conclusion.
- **Credibility (C):** "Proof that you've achieved the result that others also want." Examples: teeth-whitening chain with before/after smiles wall-to-wall; Alex and Leila selling Gym Launch to private equity for **$46.2 million** — "Same people the day before or the day after, but now they have the proof... their brand took off." The 36,000-square-foot building: "hard to fake, right? It's credibility. He must have money. He bought the building."
- **Likeness (L):** "You are similar to them" — two modes: **visual or values**. The lumberjack story: a logger asked for a photo with Alex; the realization — "lumberjacks love you... because you look like one. He literally looks like a lumberjack and he makes all the money. So they see him; they're like, it's possible." When you ARE the avatar of who you educate and sell to, it works extremely well; as the company grows you need more avatars.

**Case study — Martha Stewart (has all four → wins):** "The GOAT of all time." She gave **step-by-step recipe instructions for decades**; millions of women followed the steps and got the result they wanted. "So when she started selling stuff that was adjacent for the audience, they bought it without hesitation." Built a **$1.6 billion empire on free recipes** — cookware, food, kitchen products. She had all four pillars: status (skills, money, experience), power (decades of say-do correspondence), credibility (results, repeatedly), likeness (she looks just like the avatar).

**Case study — Charli D'Amelio (status + likeness only → fails):** TikTok-famous early on Musical.ly, **hundreds of millions of followers**; "millions of teens felt Charli was just like them." She tried selling **perfume, heels, makeup — all failed. Every single time.** Why: "She has status and likeness... but she has absolutely no credibility in the things she's selling and absolutely no power. She's never given an instruction that helped someone with life with her content." Room exercise — what SHOULD she sell? Dancing ("she's a world-class dancer — dancingschool.com or something").

**The verdict for educator-type businesses:** "Status and likeness — cool, if you have it, but it's really the **power and credibility** that you need. Those are the two that matter most."

**Big brand events:** Why does Red Bull do stratosphere jumps? "Red Bull gave this guy the energy to jump from space. It's probably enough to get you through the meeting... statistically, more of you will get a Red Bull from the refrigerator." Same logic: "If this North Face jacket can keep him warm on Mount Everest... from the car to the door, he'll be warm." And: "If the Hormozis sold $160 million in three days and became a Guinness World Record holder, they could probably help you with sales and marketing." Company expression: **"Show what only you can show and say what only you can say."**

**Brand's unique economic property:** "Brand is the only thing in the world that I know that over time **lowers your cost to acquire customers**. It's the only thing I am aware of. Our cost to acquire new customers every quarter has gone down because the brand has gotten bigger." Supporting story: a 19-year-old single-product ecom client in a knockoff-saturated market ("literally the same product from China knockoffs on Amazon for half the price") was told "I need you to do like 100x more videos"; he did, made millions more, and his CAC keeps dropping despite cheaper competitors — "because they have no brand. He has brand." And the AI kicker: "With AI becoming everything, we trust less. So you have to do the things that build more trust, which is personal [content]... Alex goes live more often now — thirty days of live — because **AI can't do that yet**. The things that only you can do and show, we have to do."

### 3. The master content framework: Proof → Promise → Plan (+ Pain, Picture)

"Our master framework for creating presentations, video sales letters, emails, landing pages, paid ads — you name it — organic content... five steps make content stack." The trifecta plus two superchargers:

1. **Proof** — the first question every viewer asks, consciously or not: **"Why should I listen to this person?"** Alex states who he is and that Acquisition.com does a quarter of a billion dollars a year **in every video** — "He doesn't want to, he's bored... but you have to say the proof part because we assume that no one knows anybody when they see the video, and no one's offended by the reminder." Proof = results, testimonials, case studies, screenshots, data — "everything you can show. A lot of you guys have proof; you just don't show it."
   - **Post ALL testimonials, not just the great ones:** "40 pounds lost, 80 pounds lost, 5 pounds lost, no pounds lost, body fat didn't change at all, made friends — whatever the testimonial was, I would share it every single time."
   - **Repost winners until they die:** ecom client's UGC video did 17M views → reposted → 14M → 10M → 7M → kept posting daily until it did 10K views. "He goes, 'Every time I post that, it makes sales.' **Don't be afraid to ruin your brand by sharing more proof.**"
   - Environmental proof: Gym Launch gyms had floor-to-ceiling testimonials and before/afters ("You sell the person, and they're like, 'I don't know if this will work for me.' You're like — [gestures at the wall]. Easy to sell."). Amazon's genius: "6,000+ bought in the past month," "30,000 positive reviews for durability" — "whatever you can truly prove, you got to show." The most popular menu item is the one labeled most popular — "we don't want to think for ourselves."
2. **Promise** — the viewer is thinking: "If I buy this thing / download this lead magnet, **what's going to change for me?**" Requires specific outcomes. "The promise is basically the hook of the organic content and is what stops them from scrolling."
3. **Plan** — "You have to explain how you are going to get the person to their desired result. This reduces the gap of 'I want that thing but I don't believe I can get it.'" Specific numbered steps + exact call-outs = belief. Operating posture: **"Approach content as: no one knows who I am, and no one believes me. Start there."**
4. **Pain (supercharger #1)** — "The biggest mistake people make is they try to train the new salesperson on the product they sell. It does not matter. You only train them on the prospect. Can they speak the pain points and the language of the prospect?" Everyone thinks they're a special snowflake no one understands; "the prospect feels that you understand them when you talk about their pain points."
5. **Picture (supercharger #2)** — "Our eyes can consume it way faster than our ears can... you have to have a visual that tells the story in like one second. People see before they hear, and when the visual matches the audio, it's like confirming." Applies most to thumbnails and ad visuals: "at a glance they're like, oh, clearly a blueprint to do something."

**The dissected example — Alex's #1 YouTube video of all time**, played to the room, mapped beat-by-beat:
- *Proof:* "I was able to take on more than a year's worth of CEOs at McDonald's, IKEA, Ford, Motorola and Yahoo combined as a kid in his twenties for over half a decade... which resulted in two hundred million dollars in portfolio revenue as of today, crossed one hundred million dollars by age thirty-three. And to be honest, no one's more surprised than I am about this outcome."
- *Call-out (pain/likeness filter):* "Me expressing this will create envy in some people. It will create anger in others. Skepticism in most. Confusion in old people. And inspire a select few... the select few, you are who I am making this for. Everyone else, you can chill."
- *Promise:* "Who wants to hear about some stuff [that] can shortcut their path to material success, so they can ponder the purpose of achieving it to begin with?"
- *Plan:* "We're gonna cover three things today... Number one, how making money really works. Number two, why new stuff is making you poor. Number three, why better is the way."
- Coda: "Proof, promise, plan — if you actually pay attention, it's in everything we do. Every single thing we do. And I encourage you guys to do the same."

### 4. Content CPM economics — organic posts are unspent ad dollars

Quantifying what organic content is actually worth instead of guessing:
- CPM = price for 1,000 impressions on a paid ad. **Meta CPM range: ~$15–25** (local to national; can differ internationally).
- "If you are an average local business getting **1,000 to 3,000 views on an organic post, one post could be worth $15 to $45–75** if you spent it in ad dollars. We just posted it."
- The question that follows: "Can you produce content with the same reach or more for less than $15–75 a post? Yeah — so much cheaper than it used to be." **"If you can produce organic content in quality and volume to your audience at scale, you lower the price of attention."**
- Acquisition.com's own math: **~1.2 billion impressions per quarter** across three channels (Alex, Leila, Acquisition.com) — B2B impressions. "To rent 1.2 billion impressions at a conservative $20 CPM, it'd be **$25 million a quarter — $100 million a year**. We don't spend that much on the team to make content. It's a few million bucks a year, it's not one hundred."

### 5. The highlights-channel reveal (Alex, verbatim segment)

- "There is something called the highlights channel on YouTube... **that channel is run by one guy and a bunch of AI workflows. We post about 20 times a day there.**" The videos get 800 views, 1,000 views, "some 20s, some hundreds, whatever" — ~140 videos a week.
- The punchline: **"That channel has more attributed revenue to ACQ than my main channel."**
- **"Your views and likes don't matter for how much money you make. That's like point number one."**
- Point number two (Alex): "You need to make **way more ads** than you did before, and you have to be **way more segmented in your audiences in terms of your call-outs and your messaging**... instead of making one ad for plumbers, make ten ads with slightly different slices of plumbing. You want to get as niche as you possibly can, because your cost per result is going to go down — which is a good thing — but you're going to have to burn through more of it. That's the nature of the game now."
- Volume-negates-luck comparison from later in the talk: a channel posting **once per day (Dalton) → 200,000 views monthly**; Alex's channel posting **24 times a day → 30 million views**. "Same business, just got found thirty times more."

### 6. Pre- vs post-Andromeda paid ads

Meta's Andromeda update rewrote the rules:
- **Pre-Andromeda:** "You could run a few creatives and they lasted forever. They didn't have to be that specific; they'd be general, and you'd do interest targets, et cetera."
- **Post-Andromeda:** "Way more creatives, and they last a very short amount of time. **If you are local brick-and-mortar, they burn out like right away.** If you are national, you get a couple weeks out of them maybe. They've got to be super specific now, and **the creative IS the targeting**. Content is the targeting, the creative is the targeting; it's the same game."
- The saturation myth: "Business owners constantly tell us their market is saturated... I promise you it's not. **Your creative is saturated. You don't make enough creative. That's all it is.** Every time we have this problem and then we scale up creative, their cost per lead goes down, their cost per acquired goes down."
- The multiplier, stated flat: **"If you spent the same amount of money every month on Meta pre-Andromeda [with] 15 ads, it has to be 150 now. I am not exaggerating."** And: "It's easier to make them than ever."
- Scale reference: School.com runs **~1,000 ads at any given time, rotating hundreds more per week**, each with very different call-outs — "Always wanted to play music? Join a school." / "Start your online relationship coaching business." / "Learn how to fix a stuck ecom store." / "Learn to sing." — "Look how specific these are... Volume is the answer, guys."
- Organic + paid are no longer two systems: "It used to be two different systems. It's one system now. It's one team. It's one approach." Old world: two channels, two teams, double the work, double the cost. New world: "one team, one channel, lots of content, find outliers, run those paid, keep doing it."
- Why Meta engineered it this way: "Mark Zuckerberg ain't a dummy... he does not need your marketing dollars; he needs everybody to use his platform. **He will not allow bad generic ads on his platform because it'll drive users off**... if you try to run [generic] ads like [it's] 2025, you'll lose." And the window: "Only a few moments like this come around in business life. **The founders who lean in will have an unfair advantage for the next half decade.**"

### 7. The Super Six skills

#### Skill 1 — Ad Parameter Method (hook × meat × CTA)

- **"You don't want to create a bunch of ads; you want to ASSEMBLE a bunch of ads."** Three components:
  - **Hook** — gets them to stop scrolling.
  - **Meat** — "the message that speaks to the pain point of the avatar."
  - **CTA** — what you want them to do next.
- Assembled example (ACQ's own ad): Hook — "Most entrepreneurs don't realize they're the bottleneck." Meat — every hire routes to them, every fire, the pain-point litany. CTA — "Click the link, book a call, and let's get you out of your own way." Second example: Hook — "Are you struggling to scale?" Meat — "These three things are the problem." CTA — "Just answer a few questions, we'll send you a free scaling roadmap." Third: "Business owners — real quick, can I get your email? These are your problems. Download free scaling frameworks."
- **The production formula: 50 hooks × 3–5 meats × 1–3 CTAs ≈ 750 assembled ads.** "This formula is how we make seven hundred and fifty ads that we assemble. You've got to shoot these things and then you mix them, match them... **You can do one shoot per month, generate hundreds of ads.**"
- **The call-out ladder** — same hook, progressively narrower call-out: "If you own a chiropractic clinic doing a million a year, we built a roadmap for you" → one layer deeper: "If you own a **holistic** chiropractic clinic doing one million" → "If you own a **Gonstead** chiropractic clinic..." — "We just keep getting more and more granular. You've got to keep going — niche down more."
- Why hyper-specific call-outs convert (room exchange): an attendee bought a workshop ticket from an ad that called out "dentist" exactly. "Did you scroll past other ads? Probably. We all do because we're blind. It's like — not me, not me... **Oh, me! It's all about me!** That's why they click."
- Bridge rule: "If it works on organic — these are organic clips that work without money — it will crush on paid. **Next time you do a viral video and you don't run it as an ad, we're not friends anymore.**"

#### Skill 2 — Kaleidoscope (70/20/10)

"The hardest thing for entrepreneurs is to do the same thing every single day... **If you want to make more money, do the same thing every single day.** Ads is where you get to flex your creative ability."
- **70% — More (minimal variation on winners):** "If you have a winner, just add a slight tweak and do more of what's working." Change a filter or color grade; different actor, exact same script; **same actor, different t-shirt ("not joking, just change the shirt")**; same actor, different background. "The principle: **maximize leverage on proven content**. Less time, less energy, more winning. That's most of your ads, every week."
- **20% — Adjacent (meaningful variation, still close to the winner):** different testimonial, different case study, different hook, changed CTA. "The core of it is the same, but we're changing a meaningful thing... we already know this format works; we're just going to see if we can get a better hook on top of it."
- **10% — New (no guardrails):** "This is where you should go batshit crazy. Try whatever you want... brand new concepts, no guardrails... Don't change your offers — just use your brain on marketing." Budget discipline: "**Pick a number of dollars you are okay with losing and just try random shit**, because every once in a while you find a super winner... assume you'll just burn the money." When the 10% finds a winner, it feeds back into the flywheel: "New winner → 70% more of them, 20% adjacent, 10% new. Keep cycling. **This will lower your cost to acquire by a lot.**"

#### Skill 3 — The 10-to-1 rule

- The mistake: "You guys get bored of your content, so you make up new shit for the sake of variety and novelty **because you are bored**." The secret of every influencer: "Alex doesn't want to make another two-and-a-half-hour sales video. He doesn't want to do it, but it works... You've got to keep doing the thing that works." The Ford marketing-director line: **"We get bored before the market does."** Dave Ramsey hasn't said anything new in thirty years; Gary [Vee] hasn't in twenty — "they find slightly new ways to say it, but they're saying the same thing."
- The right way: **one big idea, backed by ten reasons — "repetition with variety."** Five types of writing to vary the same lesson: **stories, examples, metaphors, data, statistics.**
- The worked example — big idea: **"You should all market more."** Ten reasons:
  1. **Most businesses die unknown** — "they don't die from being bad; they die from being invisible. More businesses fight irrelevance than incompetence."
  2. **People can't buy [what] they can't find** — "no awareness is no sale. Awareness starts way before offers and sales; being known comes first."
  3. **Volume negates luck** — Dalton posts once/day → 200K views/month; Alex posts 24×/day → 30M views. "Same business, just got found thirty times more."
  4. **Double your leads, double your business** — "if everything else is equal... twice the leads, twice the business, every time."
  5. **If you're down to your last dollar, spend it on marketing** — "if you don't spend that on getting found, nothing else will save you." (Healthcare SaaS client story, below.) "When in doubt, buy attention."
  6. **Share of voice leads to share of market** — "if you are ten percent of the ads in your category, you will be ten percent of the sales over time. Loud share becomes market share." Gym experience: prospects converted in month six on the same offer they'd seen for six months — "they don't believe you're legit until they see it a lot of times."
  7. **Awareness decays the second you stop paying for it** — "**Coca-Cola spends four billion a year on ads.** Everyone knows them; they still pay. When you stop being seen, you start getting forgotten."
  8. **The longer they see you, the more they choose you** — "**Geico went from number six to number two in auto insurance. They outspent everybody for twenty years** to do it."
  9. **Even in a downturn** — "every recession since 1920 tells the same story: the brands that spent more when shit was bad grew market share... and kept it for years afterwards." COVID story: Gym Launch told all clients to **10x ad spend** in March 2020; the ones who listened got "**dollar clicks, forty-two-cent clicks**; they were like twenty [dollars]... Was it harder to close somebody in lockdown? Yeah, but it was **one-eighteenth the price**. When everyone gets scared, you get found cheap."
  10. **Marketing today is sales tomorrow** — "getting found is a leading indicator; revenue is a lagging indicator... **Dig the well before you're thirsty.**"
- Challenge to the room: "Don't come up with new ideas; [come up with] more reasons and support."

#### Skill 4 — Five levels of awareness × five hook types

"Most of you guys have only hit the easy two. To break through, you need all five. To get a hundred-million-dollar company, you've got to be amazing at all five." (Flagged as mattering most above $25K/month spend, "but everybody should know it.") Based on the famous book [Breakthrough Advertising], which "Alex made better by saying these are the five hooks that go with the five levels of awareness":

| Awareness level | State of the prospect | Hook type | Transcript examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unaware** | "No idea about anything" — don't even know they have a problem; you must educate them that one exists | **Curiosity-driven** | "The hidden danger in your daily routine that's costing you money, time and health"; "the thing you don't know that might be poisoning you in your house"; "you might be overpaying for energy, insurance, etc."; **the mesothelioma commercials — "If you worked at this place from this time to this time, you might have mesothelioma." It's that shape.** |
| **Problem aware** | Know they have a problem, don't know a solution exists | **Pain-driven** | "Tired of [painful problem]? There is a better way — let me educate you how"; "Is it exhausting always being exhausted? New solution found." — "You've got to dig into their pain." |
| **Solution aware** | Know solutions exist; evaluating options ("Which one do I buy?") | **Promise-driven** — "This is your grand slam offer. This is where you hit them with the offer." | "The fastest way to get [the thing] — introducing XYZ thing that I sell." |
| **Product aware** | Know YOUR solution, asking "Are they better? Is this the right one?" | **Proof-driven** — "It's all proof." | "[18] people just like [avatar type] who [solved problem] in [timeframe]"; "the battle-tested marketing solution for dentists who need more leads — see case study below." |
| **Most aware** | "They just [need] the reason to buy" | **Offer-driven** | "ACQ just launched its first marketing workshop. Here you are. You guys already know us." — "It's just a new offer in the marketplace. That's it." |

Mnemonic: **unaware → curiosity, problem → pain, solution → promise, product → proof, most aware → offer.**

#### Skill 5 — Leveraging "NEW"

- "The two most powerful words in all marketing are **'free' and 'new.'**" (This session covers "new"; "we do a lot on free but that's a whole other thing.")
- Rule #1: **"Make as small of a change as possible to something that already works."** Take the stale-but-proven offer and remix the wrapper, not the delivery: Gym Launch — "Fall Six-Week Challenge" = "Spring Twenty-Eight-Day Blueprint" — "same exact delivery, just a different wrapper. **Same bundle, different wrapper.**" Chief/Sheaf Labs supplements: "Six-Week Nutrition Challenge" → "28-Day Slim Down" → "**Lean by Halloween**" → "**Slim Down for Santa**" — same supplements every time. "It's the same core delivery, but **people need an excuse to do it again.**"
- The workshop itself is a meta-example: scaling workshop → marketing workshop — "the content's all different; the structure of the two days is the same. I'm not going to change the structure that already works."
- Valid "reasons for new": seasonal, monthly special, someone's birthday, a new office — "somebody had their tooth pulled; doesn't matter. You have to have a reason to do something new."
- **The grand-reopening move:** "When I had gyms, every single year I had a grand reopening — just because lead cost went down every time. So every October, grand reopening." Closing law: **"Good reasons [are] better than bad reasons; bad reasons [are] better than no reason. Got to have a reason."**

#### Skill 6 — The organic→paid flywheel

- The three steps: **(1) Post lots of organic — "don't overthink it, just post it." (2) Look for outliers — "one-of-tens," the top 1-out-of-10 videos. (3) Run the outlier as a paid ad.** "That's the game. You used to have to spend money to test a bunch of paid ads to see which one worked. **Now it's free.**" Then recycle: "The platform tested [it] for you, you take the winners, run them on paid channels, get more organic content, and you do it again and again."
- **The $100M book-launch story (the proof):** For the "$100M" book launch (August 16, one year prior), Alex **shot 4,000 new unique ads over a few months** — "You didn't hear me: four thousand new ones" — including running on the Vegas strip with a giant book ("he's not a runner — he's not built [for it] — did it anyway"). The result: **"The best-performing ads for the entire book launch were NOT from the four thousand bangers he scripted and made. Every single top-performing, revenue-driving ad came from organic posts that [had gone viral] on podcasts — that the algorithm already said people love."** Millions of views for zero dollars. The team took those battle-tested organic clips and **"added one single line to the end of the clip: 'If you like that, I have a new book. Click here.'** And those outperformed everything he shot."
- Moral: "This is where organic content at scale makes you lots of money. This is why it's easier than ever before — except you have to go hard. You used to spend so much split-testing and guessing; now it does it for you."
- Session TL;DR (verbatim): "If your ads hit a wall or your cost-per is getting too expensive: **the market is not saturated. Please, please believe me. Your creative is saturated** — and if that's not the case, **your awareness is saturated**: you are only talking to people at certain points of the awareness train. Organic is your testing ground. The Andromeda update's fucking amazing — don't think it's negative; you've just got to play the game the right way."
